In the morning, the Institute sent them permission to enter something which, according to that same Institute, did not exist.

Kael discovered the contradiction before breakfast and consequently greeted his food without his usual investigative interest. Three sheets lay on the table, received through the district communications node: an updated classification of the Estel incident, a temporary extension of Mirael’s team’s authority, and an archival reference concerning the facility labelled ‘Node 7’ on the old map. The first two documents were thoroughly Elgarian in spirit: dry, detailed, and cautious enough to ensure that, in the event of a catastrophe, no one could prove that the Administration had previously promised there would be no catastrophe. The third was considerably more interesting.

Node 7 was absent from the current state register.

It was not marked ‘dismantled.’

It was not listed as ‘decommissioned.’

The registration number assigned to the facility on the old plan had officially never been issued.

Kael compared the seals again.

“This is becoming offensive,” he said.

Ollan, who was trying to persuade a piece of bread to accept far too much butter, did not even look up.

“The document?”

“The principle.”

“Which one? Bureaucracy has plenty.”

“The archival map says the facility existed. The modernisation log mentions it as having closed before work began. The current register says there never was such a number. In other words, state documents are no longer arguing about the facility’s condition, but about whether it was ever born.”

Ilsa pulled one of the copies towards her.

“Perhaps the number changed?”

“Then there would be a transfer record. Even when a municipality moves a village accumulator to another street, it generates three forms, two permits, and at least one person complaining that the old address was more convenient.”

“Are you defending bureaucracy?”

“I’m defending consistency.”

Beside them, Taven was reading the archival sheet more closely than anyone. After a few seconds, he touched the date.

“The Node’s closure is recorded four years before the network’s general modernisation. If it was retired earlier, they might simply not have transferred it to the new register.”

Kael shook his head.

“Then there should be a decommissioning certificate. Or a dismantling record. Or at least an official explanation for its removal from the plan. They didn’t remove it. They seem to have stopped counting it.”

Ollan finally looked at the papers.

“Perhaps Node 7 was the first thing in Elgarian history to realise that the best way to avoid state supervision is not to register.”

Kael was about to answer when Mirael entered the room carrying a small metal case. She set it on the free edge of the table, opened the catches, and took out a device the size of a thick book. A flat glass plate occupied its centre, with four thin mana contacts around the edges.

“The category has been raised,” she said. “Officially, it’s now Active Deviation II. Until the anomaly’s nature is established, any excursion beyond yesterday’s cordon requires a Signature check before entry and after return.”

Ollan looked at the device.

“A resonator? We spent all yesterday beside that thing, and now the Administration remembers the rules?”

“Yesterday, the object was classified as a local residual manifestation. Today, we have an effect on modern navigation and unknown infrastructural connectivity. If you’d like to challenge the protocol, I’ll gladly attach your complaint to the report after the check.”

“I suddenly have a profound respect for protocol.”

Mirael slid the resonator towards him.

“Then we’ll begin with the most respectful.”

Hellen’s instrument was one of those Elgarian inventions that looked far too simple for the amount of theory concealed inside. A person placed a palm on the glass; the four contacts supplied a weak mana pulse, barely enough to touch the soul’s outer structure; and the device read the characteristics of the returning flow. For most people, the result was almost flat. The soul accepted mana, redistributed it, and returned it without any noticeable interaction with archē.

Sometimes, something else happened.

Elgarion called it a Signature.

Not an Ability, not a blessing, and certainly not a mark of being chosen. According to accepted theory, a small amount of archē sometimes intervened in a soul’s structure during its formation, leaving a stable pattern. Why this happened only in some newborns, the Institute still did not know. A bearer could live an entire life scarcely noticing the Signature. In one person, it manifested as an unusual sense of mana’s direction; in another, as excessively precise distance perception; in a third, as an unpleasant ability to know beforehand which part of a spell was about to collapse. Some Signatures were so vague that for decades they remained nothing more than a pretty diagram in a medical archive.

But Elgarion had gathered enough data to identify a pattern: if a Signature bearer ever reached Awakening, their principal Ability almost always developed in the same direction.

One of Kael’s instructors had once called a Signature the soul’s handwriting before it learned to write complete sentences. The formulation was unacademic, which was why every student remembered it better than the official one.

Ollan placed his palm on the plate. Several thin luminous lines filled the glass. Instead of forming the usual closed pattern, they spread apart and rejoined where, geometrically, they should not have been able to meet.

“Unchanged,” Mirael said, consulting the previous readings. “Open Circuit is stable.”

“Glad to hear my soul still hasn’t learned to follow the diagram.”

Even before Awakening, Ollan had possessed an unusual trait: his mana passed between incompatible conductors more easily than it should. Not more powerfully, not faster; it simply found a way around places where a normal flow stopped. After Awakening, that tendency became an Ability the Institute classified as ‘temporary replacement of disrupted mana-circuit connectivity with one’s own soul channel.’

Ollan called it Shunt.

Kael had once called it a ‘biological emergency cable.’

Unresolved terminological disagreements had existed between them ever since.

Ilsa was next. Her pattern consisted of several gentle pulsations matching her heartbeat while simultaneously reacting to the people around her. Somatic Resonance had allowed her, even before Awakening, to sense other people’s physiological disturbances as faint discomfort in her own body. Awakening brought Compensation: Ilsa could briefly synchronise her mana with another person’s body and redistribute part of its internal imbalance. She could not heal wounds with a touch or bring the dead back to life, but she could keep a heart from stopping, reduce shock, stabilise damaged mana circulation, or buy a few minutes where, without her, a person would not have had even those.

The price was simple and unpleasant: for a time, Ilsa’s own body took on part of the burden.

Taven was third to place his palm on the resonator. A thin rectangular grid spread across the glass. Several lines shifted slightly, then returned to their original positions.

“Metric Shadow shows no deviations,” Mirael said.

Taven nodded as though receiving an ordinary weather forecast.

His Signature had had almost no practical value before Awakening. As a child, he simply sensed when a room ‘didn’t add up.’ A room could look perfectly normal and a measuring tape show correct values, but Taven felt an almost physical irritation if the distance between two points somehow stopped corresponding to its own geometry. After Awakening, that trait became Reference Grid: a limited region within which Taven could establish his own standard of measurement. He did not correct a distortion, but making ten metres pretend to be nine became considerably harder in his presence.

“Kael,” Mirael said.

He placed his palm on the glass.

The pulse passed through his skin almost imperceptibly. A thin chain of branching lines appeared inside the plate. They never looked the same, but always followed one principle: one line drew out the next, and that one another, until the entire structure resembled a drawing someone had constructed from dependencies rather than shapes.

“Coupled Sequence is stable,” Mirael reported. “Slight amplification of the third circuit, but within your normal range.”

Ollan looked at the diagram.

“Every time I see that, it looks as though your soul has made a list of reasons it doesn’t want to work with us.”

“It looks more like a structured objection to the existence of chaos.”

“That’s exactly what I said, only in human language.”

Kael did not answer immediately.

His Signature had been discovered when he was thirteen, though its first manifestations must have begun earlier. Until then, he had grown up in an ordinary family in western Elgarion: his father maintained municipal mana networks, and his mother taught applied mathematics at a technical school. Kael’s childhood included no murdered mentors, hidden prophecies, or mysterious origins, which in retrospect he considered one of his great advantages in life. Far more often, it included dismantled instruments, punishments for dismantled instruments, and explanations of why an instrument had to be reassembled before its owner noticed the instrument was missing.

The incident that led to his examination happened in a teaching laboratory. One of the demonstration converters was overheating. The teacher was looking for the fault in the accumulator, the other students were arguing about damage to the main channel, and Kael, for some reason, was watching a small side stabiliser that showed no sign of malfunction.

He said the cause was there.

The teacher asked what, precisely, he had noticed.

Kael could not answer.

The stabiliser burst less than a minute later.

No one was hurt, but a week later an Institute specialist arrived at the school and asked Kael a question he remembered to this day.

“Did you see the damage, or the connection?”

At the time, the distinction had seemed almost pedantic.

Later, it determined his profession.

After Awakening, Kael’s Signature unfolded into Coupling. He could consciously trace a chain of dependencies within a system he understood sufficiently well: see which circuit fed a barrier, which part of a movement produced the next strike, which fault in one element would cause a failure in another. The Ability did not show the future or reveal truth. The less Kael understood an object, the more Coupling filled the gaps with noise, possible connections, and false sequences. Overload brought a migraine, then a loss of the sense of event order; if he continued long enough, the blood vessels in his nose reminded him that a human head had not been designed to observe hundreds of causal relationships simultaneously.

Kael considered those limitations entirely reasonable.

Ollan called them the only evidence that the world occasionally defended itself against Kael.

Mirael checked herself last. Her diagram was almost motionless: a broad inner circuit maintained its shape despite the resonator’s changing pressure. Reference State. Even as a child, Mirael had slightly stabilised mana constructs nearby. A negligible effect, useful for little beyond laboratory observation. After Awakening, it became Anchor: the ability temporarily to establish the current state of several selected objects as a reference for her own mana. Anchor did not make people invulnerable, but it substantially increased their resistance to spatial displacement, sudden mana impacts, and attempts by an anomaly to alter something Mirael had already decided to regard as a starting point.

Kael had once observed that her Ability was suspiciously well suited to the position of field curator.

She had replied that, regrettably, the Institute did not appoint leaders according to a poetic correspondence between soul and profession.

Then she had made him rewrite his report anyway.

Mirael closed the case.

“Readings are stable. If anyone feels a change in their own Signature or Ability inside the Node, report it immediately, even if the change seems useful. Especially if it seems useful.”

“Was that last remark directed at anyone in particular?” Ollan asked.

“At everyone who asks that question.”

Before leaving, Kael lingered over the documents. The Signature-check form contained a separate line: ‘Religious intervention, blessings, external alterations of the soul.’ Beside it was a field for recording Gifts of the Triunity.

He ran a finger over the wording.

Ilsa noticed.

“Another word you don’t like?”

“This time, it isn’t the word itself.”

The Triunity called personal manifestations Gifts, bestowed on people by deities according to its doctrine. Not necessarily at birth. A Gift could be granted later, in recognition of faith, service, or a special affinity with a particular cult. Elgarian researchers had spent decades arguing over whether a Gift could be compared with a Signature at all. The findings were inconvenient for both sides: some structures really did appear related, but the manner of their emergence differed too greatly.

“I’ve always wondered why they consider it a reward,” Kael said.

Ollan slung his bag over his shoulder.

“Because religion likes the word ‘reward.’ It sounds better than ‘unpredictable soul anomaly.’”

“A Signature is not an anomaly in the pathological sense.”

“That was exactly the correction I was waiting for.”

Kael ignored him.

“If the structure really is related, one oddity remains. Here, someone either is born with a Signature or isn’t. They can learn to understand it; after Awakening, they can develop the corresponding Ability. But the Signature itself doesn’t appear out of thin air at thirty just because someone has served the state well.”

Ilsa fastened her medical bag.

“Where are you going with this?”

“Nowhere yet. There’s a hypothesis I don’t like.”

“Then you’re definitely going to tell us.”

Kael looked at the line about Gifts again.

“Suppose newborns in the Triunity experience the same initial contact between soul and archē. Then the difference might lie not in the phenomenon’s nature, but in what happens after birth. If that influence can be blocked, suppressed, or even partially extracted before it becomes fixed, the Signature won’t manifest. Later, when someone becomes useful enough or faithful enough, the structure can be returned or unblocked, and it can be announced that a god has granted them power.”

Ollan stopped smiling.

So did Ilsa.

Mirael, standing by the door, answered first:

“Evidence?”

“None.”

“Then remember that thought exactly that way.”

“I said it was a hypothesis.”

“Good. Because if it’s true, it’s a system of control on a scale Elgarion ought to know about. And if it isn’t, you’ve just invented a very convincing way to accuse an allied state of spiritual selection among infants.”

Kael nodded.

“Which is why it stays in my head for now, rather than in a report.”

Ollan headed for the exit.

“I hate it when you start with ‘suppose.’ Afterwards, either an instrument breaks or diplomacy does.”

“Statistically, the latter has happened less often.”

“Because they still rarely let you speak to diplomats.”

They reached Node 7 in two hours.

The old route slipped into the forest almost unnoticed. At first, only the archival map kept them on course, but the farther they moved from the field, the more small signs appeared that could not be dismissed as coincidence. Young trees grew on either side of the former road but scarcely encroached on its centre. Branches met above the path, leaving a narrow clear corridor between them. After the night’s rain, water continued collecting in shallow longitudinal ditches unsupported by the terrain’s present geometry.

Mana was behaving incorrectly too.

In an ordinary forest, it was unevenly distributed: collecting around roots, flowing along damp earth, lingering a little more densely near living things. Here, small background flows periodically stretched along the vanished road, as though the old infrastructure still offered them a more convenient route.

Several times, Kael tried to catch the pattern with Coupling, but did not go deep. He sensed only direction: the local phenomena did not exist separately. Every turn of the water, the vegetation’s behaviour, and the movement of mana reached onward towards the same point.

Taven stopped first.

“There’s a change.”

Mirael immediately raised a hand, stopping the others.

“How serious?”

Taven took out four small metal markers and placed them at the corners of the area he intended to examine. Then he closed his eyes. The mana within his body changed almost imperceptibly: no flash, no outward burst; the surrounding space simply acquired, for a moment, a sense of precision, as though someone’s invisible drawing had been laid over the forest.

Reference Grid.

Kael did not see it as a glowing dome. Nothing so convenient. But his Clarity—the second Threshold of mana perception—could distinguish the thin lines by which Taven bound his sense of distance to the markers. Within that region, every displacement acquired a standard.

Taven walked ten paces forward.

Stopped.

Returned.

Looked at Kael.

“Measured externally, this is about twenty-four metres.”

“And inside?”

“A little over twenty-seven.”

Ollan looked around at the trees.

“Am I understanding correctly that we’ve lost three metres of space, and it didn’t even bother to tell us?”

“Not lost. They exist within the route, but aren’t counted from outside.”

Kael looked at the old road.

“Retention is preserving the extent of the former infrastructure.”

“Possibly,” Taven said. “Or what the road once required for its own length.”

Ollan sighed.

“The two of you become unbearable together much faster than you do separately.”

Mirael placed an additional signal marker.

“From here on, active-zone procedure. Taven maintains geometry. No one leaves the group without an order.”

Her voice changed only slightly, but the jokes ended of their own accord.

Several hundred metres farther on, the forest ended at a rocky slope.

The archival map placed Node 7 directly in front of them.

In reality, they faced a solid wall of rock overgrown with moss and low roots.

Ollan approached and tapped the stone.

“If this is a building, the builders took camouflage very seriously.”

Taven activated his Grid again. This time he stood motionless for almost a minute.

“There’s a room.”

Ollan looked back.

“We’re facing a hillside.”

“I’m not arguing with the hillside. There’s a room behind it.”

“Entrance?”

“Here, on the archival map.”

Kael approached the wall and laid his palm on the cold stone. Mana revealed nothing. Ordinary rock. The archē needle remained almost clear too, except for one section where a thin pale crack appeared on its surface.

He decided to use Coupling.

The world did not visibly change. Coupling rarely looked impressive. Kael simply chose one question: what was the old road connected to here?

First came the familiar sense of structure.

Road.

Old guides.

Distribution flow.

Wall.

Then the connection went deeper.

Kael expected to see the Node as one element of the network.

He got the reverse.

The station depended on it.

The tower depended on it.

The distribution branches spread out through it.

Even the old road automation was, in some sense, tied to this place.

Kael abruptly stopped the Ability.

“What?” Mirael asked.

He spent several seconds gathering the sequence back into words.

“We misunderstood the architecture. The Node wasn’t another facility on the line. At least, not only that. Most of the old network referred to it as a reference element.”

Ollan looked at the rock.

“Reference in what sense?”

“I don’t know yet. But if you removed it from the plan, some connections would have had to change. Perhaps that’s why they modernised the entire network.”

Mirael noticed him brush his fingers beneath his nose.

“Overload?”

“Mild.”

“Then no second attempt unless necessary.”

Kael wanted to say he had reached the same decision himself, but her wording plainly left no room for discussion.

Meanwhile, Taven moved a few paces to one side.

“The room’s boundary is here.”

He touched the stone.

“And there should be a door here.”

Ollan laid out his tools. Beneath the upper layer of rock, they did indeed find an old metal strip. Not the door itself, but apparently part of a locking circuit that had somehow remained embedded in the present wall.

“The circuit’s dead,” he said after testing it. “Deliberately broken, too. Two key lines have been drawn apart.”

“Can you close it?”

Ollan looked at Mirael.

“In a normal facility, yes. Here, I’d really like to hear that everyone understands how bad an idea it is to use Shunt on a system that tried to teach a cart to drive along dead roads yesterday.”

“We understand,” she replied. “Which is why you begin only after Kael determines the sequence, Taven fixes the geometry, and I establish Anchor. At the first deviation, withdraw.”

“Now it sounds like a professional bad idea.”

Mirael selected the four of them and herself as Anchor’s targets. Kael felt her mana almost immediately: it did not surround his body with a protective layer, but seemed to mark his current position and condition with a heavy line. Here. Now. Like this. A simple assertion that Mirael’s soul was prepared to defend against any outside influence.

Taven extended his Grid over the entrance.

Kael traced the surviving dependencies of the locking circuit. Coupling worked much more easily here: the system was old, but comprehensible. Three consecutive pulses. Outer line. Return through the stabiliser. Then disconnection.

“The break is on the second branch,” he said. “Ollan, close it only after the first pulse. Not before. If you establish the connection immediately, the circuit will decide the door is already open, and we don’t know what it currently considers the door.”

“I love clarifications that make instructions worse.”

Ollan touched the two broken points with his palms.

His mana stretched between them.

Shunt looked simple: a thin bluish line passed through his arms and chest and emerged again, turning Ollan himself into the missing section of the circuit. For a second, his body became part of someone else’s mechanism.

Kael supplied the first pulse.

“Now.”

Ollan closed the connection.

The circuit twitched.

A dull metallic sound came from inside the rock.

A straight vertical crack ran down the stone.

Not a physical one.

First a line appeared, then the moss on either side seemed to remember that it had never grown in the middle, and the surface began losing its continuity. Within seconds, an old metal door stood before them, mottled with corrosion.

It had not opened.

It had ceased to be absent.

Ollan removed his hands.

“I’d prefer it if mechanisms stopped doing things like that so casually.”

Kael examined the door.

“Casualness is not a property of a mechanism.”

“Sometimes I forget why I talk to you.”

Inside the Node, it was dry.

That was the first wrong thing.

The second was the absence of the expected decay.

The room was clearly many years old. Dust covered the walls, metal had darkened, wooden parts had dried and split. But the equipment had survived considerably better than it should. No collapsed panels, deep corrosion, or signs of water intrusion.

Taven immediately checked the dimensions.

“Space adds up inside. For now.”

“‘For now’ is becoming far too popular today,” Ilsa remarked.

Ollan examined the nearest cabinet.

“Most circuits have no power. Some were manually broken. Not accident damage: deliberately broken. Someone removed the conductors one by one.”

Kael ran a finger across a panel. Old labels survived beneath the dust.

‘Distribution.’

‘Route Binding.’

‘Node Registration.’

The last label made him stop.

“They didn’t just distribute mana here.”

Mirael came closer.

“What else?”

“It looks like an infrastructure synchronisation system. Perhaps the Node told the other elements of the network which objects belonged to it and how they were connected.”

Ollan opened another cabinet.

“So if a road was a road to the network, the record saying it was a road was kept here?”

“In simplified terms.”

“Amazingly, I like simplified terms.”

Most of the paper archives were gone. Several drawers held only disintegrated pulp; others were empty. Someone had removed the documents long before the room became concealed in the hillside.

But an inscription remained on the inner wall beside the central panel, scratched directly into the metal.

Not a prayer.

Not a warning born of old superstitions.

A professional instruction.

“DO NOT INCLUDE THE NODE IN NEW PLANS.

DO NOT COPY THE DESIGNATION.

IF A CONNECTION IS DISCOVERED, BREAK IT MANUALLY.

REMOVING THE OBJECT DOES NOT ELIMINATE ITS FUNCTION.”

Ollan stopped searching the cabinets.

Ilsa read the text again.

“Someone who knew wrote this.”

Kael moved almost close enough to touch it.

The last line confirmed what their experiments had shown. But the first two were more important.

Do not include it in plans.

Do not copy the designation.

He looked at the empty archive drawers.

Then at the missing registration number.

“They weren’t only removing the object.”

Mirael understood before the others.

“They were removing references to it.”

“Yes.”

Kael approached the old registration panel. Several plates had been torn off. Only mounting marks remained where the Node’s number should have been.

“If Retention preserves relationships, a document is a relationship too. A map asserts that the object is here. A register asserts that it belongs to the network. An inventory number connects it to other objects. The more systems acknowledge the Node’s existence, the more connections remain.”

Ollan slowly exhaled.

“So they didn’t simply close it.”

“They were making the surrounding systems stop referring to it.”

“Destroying the infrastructure’s memory.”

Kael looked at him.

“Possibly. I wouldn’t use the word ‘memory’ yet.”

“Of course. It’s too short.”

Mirael approached the central panel.

“Then our presence here is creating new connections too.”

The sentence hung in the air with unpleasant precision.

Five Awakened.

Institute instruments.

An archival map.

A resonator among their equipment.

Reference Grid.

Coupling.

They had not merely discovered Node 7.

They had incorporated it into their own systems of description again.

Kael turned sharply towards the panel.

“Put the map away. No new designations recorded inside this room. Ollan, don’t connect anything else.”

Too late.

Somewhere beneath the floor, a relay clicked.

Then another.

Old indicators on the walls lit one after another.

Not brightly.

As routinely as the circuit at the station.

That was what Kael hated most about Retention.

The Node was not awakening like an ancient monster.

It was simply returning to work.

A blue line crossed the floor.

Ollan flinched first.

“Damn.”

The mana inside his body abruptly pulled towards the nearest panel.

Shunt activated almost reflexively, responding to the system’s disrupted connectivity. It was a mistake.

The line on the floor flared.

A flow passed through Ollan.

He doubled over, pressing a palm to his chest.

“Disconnect!” Mirael ordered.

“Trying. The circuit’s maintaining feedback.”

Ilsa was beside him already. Her hand settled on his shoulder; her mana synchronised with his internal circulation. Compensation did not remove the flow, but spread the impact, preventing any one channel from taking the full load.

Ilsa inhaled sharply.

“Too much. His left side is starting to cramp.”

“Expanding Anchor,” Mirael said.

Her Ability settled more firmly over the group.

Kael felt pressure in his chest, as though their very presence here had become a little more definite.

The five of them.

Not elements of the network.

People.

Tessen Group.

Here.

Now.

The Node seemed to disagree.

New lines lit on the walls.

Taven swore for the first time since Kael had known him.

“The exit has moved.”

Despite his spasm, Ollan raised his head.

“In what sense?”

“A very literal one. The door is three metres to the right of its own position.”

Reference Grid unfolded through the room. Kael saw Taven hold the geometric standard in place as the room’s old state began overlaying its present one. One corner split in two. A wall had two permissible lengths. The door existed both where they had entered and where, according to the old plan, it had stood seventeen years earlier.

Kael activated Coupling.

The first layer was clear.

Ollan connected to a broken branch.

The branch to the distribution circuit.

The circuit to the registration panel.

The panel to the system of external nodes.

Kael went farther.

Station.

Tower.

Road.

Pylon.

The mana-mechanical cart that had entered the old transport logic yesterday.

Water.

Animals.

Each connection looked like a separate thread, but all converged here.

He expected a centre.

Instead, he found another arrow.

Down.

Deeper than the Node.

Deeper than the hillside.

Deeper than the infrastructure itself.

Kael tried to follow it.

The world instantly became too large.

For a second, he saw not lines but an impossible number of dependencies: the road existed because the nodes recognised it; the nodes existed because the system accounted for the route; the system was retained through old descriptions; the descriptions connected to the people who had made them; the people to places; the places to something else far beyond the engineering diagram.

Connections began breeding connections.

Kael tasted blood.

And switched off Coupling.

Not one more step.

Not ‘almost understood.’

Simply off.

The world returned to five people in an old room.

Blood really was running from his nose.

Mirael noticed.

“What did you see?”

Kael wiped it with his sleeve.

“The Node isn’t the source. Everything goes farther underground. I tried to read the next dependency and got too many systems at once.”

“Can you identify the way out?”

“Yes. But it isn’t the Node we need to disconnect.”

Ollan spoke through clenched teeth:

“It would be wonderful to hear that part sooner.”

Kael looked at the old panels.

“It’s working because we restored too many relationships. The map. Our instruments. Shunt. The Grid. Even registration by the Institute. If we destroy the equipment, we might create another state that Retention will preserve as well.”

Mirael understood.

“We need to remove ourselves from the system.”

“Exactly.”

She immediately began issuing orders.

“Taven, find the boundary of the present geometry and hold only that. Release the rest. Ilsa, stay on Ollan. Kael, disconnection sequence. We shift Anchor to the group and the exit. Nothing else here gets fixed in place.”

Taven shut down one of the four spatial markers. The room jerked like an image in a badly adjusted instrument.

“The present door is to the right. Two metres forty.”

“Understood.”

Kael quickly reviewed the connections they had created, now without Coupling.

First the archival marker placed outside.

Then Ollan’s portable detector.

Then the Grid.

The map last: it contained the most direct assertion that Node 7 belonged to the network.

“Ollan, don’t stop Shunt immediately. Reduce the flow to a minimum first, otherwise the branch will try to close itself through the nearest living source.”

“Very glad to learn that before becoming the nearest living lump of charcoal.”

“Ilsa, maintain his circulation for a few more seconds.”

“I am. But after we get out, he owes me two dinners and a new glove.”

“Why a glove?”

“Because this one is currently melting on his shoulder.”

Ollan looked.

“Fair.”

Even now, Kael noticed that talking helped everyone maintain their rhythm. Not jokes for laughter’s sake. Simply a familiar language reminding them that they remained people, not elements in a diagram.

He switched off the first instrument.

One line on the wall went dark.

The second.

Another disappeared.

Taven narrowed his Grid to the corridor between the group and the room’s present boundary. The walls stopped doubling, though the far corner still lived in two versions.

“Ollan, now.”

He broke Shunt.

The impact threw him backwards, but Mirael held Anchor, and Ilsa immediately stabilised his internal flow. The central line dimmed.

Kael raised the old map.

Node 7 was marked on it as a small rectangle.

He folded the sheet in half.

One of the wall circuits disappeared inside the room.

Not went dark.

Disappeared.

As though the line had never been drawn.

Kael froze for a fraction of a second.

“Again,” Taven said.

“What?”

“Fold it.”

Kael folded the map a second time.

Part of the panel’s markings vanished together with the image of the road.

Pale and still breathing heavily, Ollan said:

“I never thought I’d see a battle with infrastructure where the main weapon was a neatly folded sheet of paper.”

“Don’t distract him,” Ilsa said. “For the first time, his love of documents is saving our lives.”

Kael folded the map completely.

The Node went dark.

Not gradually.

Every active line vanished at once.

Taven pointed right.

“The exit is here.”

Mirael led the way.

A narrow opening appeared where a metal wall had stood a minute earlier. They went through one by one. Kael was last and glanced back for a moment.

The central room already looked dead.

No light.

No movement.

The scratched instruction still remained on the inner metal wall.

And below it, almost at floor level, he noticed another line.

Very old.

Almost erased.

Kael managed to read it once.

Then Mirael seized his shoulder.

“Out.”

He went out.

The door disappeared a few seconds later.

The rocky slope became solid again.

For a while, the group simply sat beneath the trees.

Ollan leaned back against a trunk with his hands stretched before him. His fingers trembled slightly. Ilsa checked his responses while trying discreetly to relax her own left arm: Compensation had taken some of the spasms upon itself.

Mirael noticed both.

“Condition?”

“Ollan will live,” Ilsa replied. “For the next few hours, don’t connect him to anything more complicated than a kettle.”

“I object to this restriction on my professional activity.”

“You may connect to a kettle.”

“That’s better.”

Mirael turned to Kael.

“And you?”

He wiped his nose again. The bleeding had almost stopped.

“Mild Coupling overload. Orientation normal, event sequence steady.”

“You went too deep.”

“And stopped before the critical threshold.”

“I know. Which is why this conversation is happening here, rather than in Elgarion’s medical department.”

Her voice contained neither praise nor irritation. Only assessment.

That was precisely why Kael took it more seriously than any reprimand.

“Next time, I won’t go beyond the local chain without an external limiter.”

“For now, there will be no next time without the Collegium’s sanction.”

Mirael took out her official transmitter.

The Institute of Reverse Circuitry was not an ordinary ministry. It had been founded as a semi-autonomous body precisely because anomalies rarely respected the pace of government approvals. At the top stood the Central Collegium of Circuits: several specialists from different disciplines, rather than a single head who could declare half the country infected by the Underside on a whim. Specialist directorates worked beneath the Collegium: Topology handled spatial disturbances; Mana Structures dealt with energy and magical systems; Somatic Deviations studied effects on living beings; Archē Analytics examined disruptions of deeper laws; Stabilisation stepped in when research ended and something had to be closed, isolated, or destroyed.

As a field curator, Mirael had broad authority on site. She could seal off a local area, requisition municipal archives, shut down a technical facility, or halt any experiment conducted by her team. But evacuating a town, large-scale destruction, or the use of particularly hazardous measures required a higher decision.

Now she exercised a power she rarely used.

The Archē Isolation Protocol.

“Tessen Group, Estel site,” she said into the transmitter. “Confirming Active Deviation with functional effects on living and technical systems. Historical documentation discovered indicating prior deliberate containment of the facility. Unstable spatial connectivity and attempted incorporation of Awakened personnel into an infrastructural circuit recorded. Requesting temporary isolation of the district and a representative of the Central Collegium.”

The transmitter accepted the message.

A copy went automatically to the district administration.

And, under the allied protocol on dangerous archē manifestations, to the Triunity.

Kael recalled the name he had heard on the road.

Aerind.

It seemed meeting their representatives was now a matter of time.

As they prepared to leave, Ollan noticed that Kael was still holding his small notebook.

“Don’t tell me you managed to write anything down inside.”

“No.”

“Then why do you look as though you’ve found another problem?”

Kael opened the last page.

Just before leaving, he had memorised the faded line. Now he reconstructed it from memory.

*“IF THE NODE BEGINS TO BE COUNTED AS THE SEVENTH AGAIN—DO NOT LOOK FOR THE EIGHTH.”*

Ollan read it.

So did Ilsa.

Taven silently unfolded the archival map, though they all knew what they would see.

The plan showed seven nodes.

Only seven.

“Perhaps it’s a professional joke,” Ilsa suggested.

“A very bad one,” Ollan said. “Though after meeting engineers from seventeen years ago, I’m no longer sure of anything.”

Mirael looked at Kael.

“We’re not looking.”

He nodded.

“There’s no basis yet for considering an eighth object real.”

Ollan smiled wryly.

“‘Yet’?”

Kael looked at the sealed hillside.

At the forest.

At the road that did not exist, yet still brought water, animals, mana, and modern mechanisms here.

Elgarion’s Institute was accustomed to classifying the unknown. First observation. Then a term. Then a mechanism. Given enough measurements, a system would eventually emerge; from that system came the ability to act upon the problem.

Kael had thought that way since he was thirteen.

But Node 7 had left an unpleasantly different thought behind.

Sometimes an object disappeared from catalogues not because it had been forgotten.

Sometimes a map was deliberately left uncopied.

A name was erased because the name itself perpetuated a connection.

And if earlier specialists had truly understood this before they did, their solution had been almost unnatural for Elgarion: do not investigate deeper, do not refine the description, do not produce a more precise classification.

Stop counting.

On the way back, Kael took out his notebook and wrote:

*“The retention effect can preserve more than physical states and functional connectivity. A formalised description of an object may also serve as a relationship supporting its participation in a system.”*

Below that, he wanted to add:

*“An observer’s memory may have a similar effect.”*

The pencil stopped.

There was no evidence.

Kael crossed out the blank line without writing it.

A hypothesis could be elegant, frightening, even probable.

That did not make it a fact.

He turned the page and added to his personal list:

*“42. Reason why Node 7 had to cease being counted as Node 7.”*

Then he thought for a moment and wrote the next entry.

*“43. The Eighth.”*

Kael looked at the word.

Crossed it out.

Walking beside him, Ollan noticed.

“A sensible decision.”

“I haven’t decided anything.”

“For the first time, you voluntarily crossed out a question.”

“I crossed out the name of an object whose existence is unconfirmed.”

“Kael.”

“What?”

“Sometimes you manage to ruin even a beautiful moment.”

Kael closed the notebook.

Behind them, the forest slowly concealed the hillside.

Somewhere deep inside it was a technical node absent from the registers, which had attempted to resume its work the moment people acknowledged it as part of the world again.

That was unpleasant enough already.

So Kael decided, at least until new data arrived, to follow the one instruction from the old engineers whose meaning he understood completely.

He would not look for the eighth node.

The problem was that no one had yet proved the eighth node needed anyone to look for it.
