By morning, Kael Reven had concluded that the inn at Estel offered the Institute of Reverse Circuitry a far more accessible subject of research than the abandoned distribution station. At least its anomalous properties manifested consistently. The water the proprietor stubbornly called hot cooled somewhere between the kitchen and the second floor; the wooden frame in Kael’s room produced the same whistle regardless of the window’s position; and the egg served for breakfast occupied such an intermediate state between cooking and a threat to public health that he spent the first ten seconds examining it with almost professional interest.

Ollan noticed where he was looking and moved his own plate out of reach in advance.

“Don’t even start. It’s a fried egg. I’ve seen fried eggs before, and I’m prepared to testify.”

“You apply names far too readily to things that clearly do not belong to the stated category.”

“Kael, if you give breakfast a designation consisting of six nouns, I’m eating your portion too.”

Across the table, Ilsa examined Kael’s plate with an entirely serious expression.

“Actually, I’d listen. I want to know how far he gets before saying the word ‘egg.’”

Kael was about to explain that a correct classification of the food item would be considerably shorter than either of them supposed when Mirael entered the common room. She carried a flat grey folder bearing the Institute’s seal, and breakfast instantly lost most of its research value.

“Elgarion replied during the night,” she said, taking the empty seat. “Central Administration doesn’t yet consider the incident dangerous enough for a full cordon, but the district has been placed under Category Three observation. We’re authorised to continue fieldwork and examine everything that belonged to the old distribution network.”

Kael accepted the papers. A fresh Administration stamp adorned the first page, followed by a list of instructions: restrict civilian access to the station, continue measurements, do not dismantle the activating circuit until further data is obtained, and inspect connected elements of the former infrastructure. Kael read that final point twice, then pushed his plate away for good.

The station had never existed in isolation. Before modernisation, one of the regional mana lines had passed through it: a network that supplied several settlements with power, carried signals, and maintained road automation. Mana in Elgarion had long since ceased to be the exclusive province of mages. People used it much as they used a good workshop fire or running water: channelled it through lines, stored it in cells, converted it into heat, light, and motion. The simplest explanation Kael had ever heard, from an elderly instructor, was almost indecently crude: mana was the filling. It let the world do something.

Archē was different.

Archē was not another kind of fuel, and generally resisted attempts to describe it in everyday words. If mana was the filling, archē was the bone. It lay beneath familiar magical processes and determined the very possibility of relationships between things: what it meant to be here, to follow after, to have a boundary, to retain a shape, or to occupy a particular place. A mage could direct mana into metal and heat it. A specialist in the Underside of the World was interested in why heated metal remained the same metal at all, and why an action performed a minute ago counted as the past rather than a continuation of the present.

That was precisely why yesterday’s circuit troubled Kael far more than an ordinary malfunction should have. There was almost no mana in it. Its physical arrangement was destroyed. Yet for several dozen seconds it returned to a state seventeen years old and performed its former functions as though the intervening years had no deciding vote.

“We need a map of the network before modernisation,” Kael said.

Without a word, Mirael drew a folded sheet from the folder.

Ollan glanced at her.

“Every time, I hope he’ll ask for something you haven’t got. Just once.”

“Then I’d have to start doing my job badly solely for your entertainment.”

“I don’t want it that much.”

Taven had already cleared the centre of the table and carefully unfolded the map. Old Estel differed from the present town more than a casual walk through its streets would suggest. The modern highway ran farther west, the stream had changed course after several engineering projects, some farmland had been reorganised, and the former service road connecting the distribution station to neighbouring facilities had almost entirely disappeared.

On the archival plan, it began at Estel, curved around a low hill, passed the station, the signal tower, and an old mana pylon, then crossed the fields towards a small wooded area.

“Yesterday, two elements of the same infrastructure manifested,” Kael said, tracing a finger from the station to the place where the tower had stood. “The circuit displayed an old configuration, and the ground bore the shadow of a structure demolished long ago. We’ve been treating them as two manifestations of one anomaly, but perhaps it would be more accurate to consider them parts of a single retained state.”

Ilsa leaned over the map.

“So you think the problem doesn’t end at the station?”

“I think we should be careful with the word ‘end’ altogether for the time being.”

Ollan sighed.

“That’s how every good field trip begins. First an old lamp lights up, then Kael starts banning verbs.”

“I banned one.”

“So far.”

Mirael folded the documents and stood.

“We’ll examine the road. Then you can argue about language as much as you like.”

Ollan looked at his unfinished breakfast.

“At last, a sensible set of priorities.”

Kael lingered for a couple of seconds, studying the archival line. Yesterday he had given the phenomenon a working name: the retention effect. Not because he understood the mechanism, but because he needed a word that did not pretend to be an answer. Retention meant holding on. For now, that was enough.

An hour later, he would regret even that modest certainty.

The old road had disappeared so conscientiously that thick wheat grew where its surface had once been. No stones, remnants of embankment, or traces of old wheel ruts remained. Taven consulted the maps several times, then stopped in the middle of the field and marked with his foot the line once followed by the service road.

“Margin of error about a metre and a half. For a seventeen-year-old district map, that’s practically a courtesy on the cartographers’ part.”

Ollan surveyed the wheat.

“If the road wants to hide, it’s doing a convincing job.”

“It isn’t hiding. It simply isn’t there,” Kael observed.

“Yes, I sometimes avoid work that way myself.”

Kael crouched and scooped a little earth into his palm. The soil was ordinary: loose, dry, repeatedly ploughed. Ollan set a manometric probe beside him and waited for the small crystal to finish its reading. The value was virtually indistinguishable from the background. Taven checked the surface gradient. Ilsa went a little farther, examining the vegetation for prolonged mana exposure. Nothing. Without the archival map in Kael’s hand, none of them would have suspected that a road had ever crossed the field.

Ilsa noticed the first oddity.

“Kael, look at the herd.”

A herd of local longshanks was approaching from the eastern side of the field: grey, long-legged mana-beasts bred locally for wool and milk. They moved slowly, showing no interest in the Institute personnel. A shepherd kept slightly to one side, leaning lazily on a long staff. At first there seemed nothing unusual about it, until Kael noticed that the animals were not heading directly towards the far fence.

They were following the vanished road.

Where the old road had curved around a small rise, the entire herd turned smoothly with it. The wheat grew equally thick on either side. There was not even a noticeable change in elevation, yet the longshanks followed the old line as precisely as though packed stone still lay beneath their hooves.

Kael stopped the shepherd and spent several patient minutes asking questions. The man was in his sixth year of working here and had never considered the animals’ behaviour unusual. The herd did indeed always cross the field by the same route, although the longshanks themselves were young, and their owner had purchased most of them in another region only two years earlier.

“Did you specifically train them to go this way?” Kael asked.

The shepherd shook his head.

“Why would I? Big field. They choose for themselves. I thought they liked the ground.”

Kael looked at the line of hoofprints, then at the map.

“The soil here is no different from the soil beside it.”

“Ask them, then. I don’t talk to them about roads.”

Ollan turned away, but the movement of his shoulders told Kael he was laughing.

The examination revealed nothing obvious. No foundation survived beneath the soil, the old materials had long since mixed with the earth, and they found no traces of guiding mana. Even the scent hypothesis seemed weak: the present herd could not have inherited a specific route from animals it had never encountered.

Kael watched for a long time as the last longshank turned after the others, then opened his notebook.

“Transgenerational behavioural adherence to a decommissioned infrastructural trajectory in the absence of a confirmed physical stimulus.”

Ilsa sighed heavily.

“What if we called it ‘animals walking along the old road’?”

“Then we would describe only the observation and lose several important features at once.”

“Such as?”

“They never knew the road. The road does not exist. And nothing detectable compels them to follow its shape.”

“Then you could have added that last sentence separately and saved half the ink.”

Beside them, Taven calmly wrote something down.

Kael turned to him.

“Again?”

“Posterity has a right to know what we had to work with.”

“The anomaly?”

Taven put his notebook away.

“You.”

This time, even Mirael smiled.

By noon, clouds had gathered over the western hills. The rain arrived quickly, but without menace: an ordinary summer shower, heavy enough to settle the dust and turn the topsoil into sticky dark mud. For the investigation, it was almost a gift. Water revealed things the eye missed: imperceptible slopes, old ditches, variations in soil density. Kael had barely thought this when the rain independently proposed a much more unpleasant experiment.

The rivulets in the field began flowing the wrong way.

At first, the deviation was so slight that it could easily have been attributed to uneven ground. Several streams reached the line of the old road, turned, and began following it. A minute later, others joined them. The water parted as though old drainage ditches still ran along both sides of the invisible road and its centre remained slightly raised.

Taven crouched, checked the surface with his frame, then repeated the measurement.

“Geometry normal. The slope runs southeast.”

Kael pointed at the water, which was flowing almost due north.

“Then either your frame has decided on a career change today, or the surface and the movement of water are using different representations of the terrain.”

“The frame is functioning. Space here is neither bent nor displaced.”

“But the water behaves as though the road exists.”

Taven examined the rivulets again and said with perfect calm:

“Space is even. Apparently the water doesn’t know.”

The statement was so unscientific that Kael almost objected automatically. He did not. For the moment, it really was the best description.

He lowered an archē needle towards the ground. Not a single characteristic notch appeared on the instrument’s dull surface. In Elgarion, the Underside of the World was the name for an entire class of phenomena in which reality’s familiar structure began behaving as though it had been turned inside out, exposing its seams. Sometimes the length of space changed; sometimes cause ceased to correspond properly to effect; sometimes a spell retained its shape after its mana was gone. The Institute existed for precisely such cases: first establish how deeply the disturbance affected archē, then decide whether it could be stabilised or whether it would be safer to remove everything connected to it.

But here, the bones of the world seemed intact.

And yet the filling moved according to a shape that no longer existed.

“Perhaps Retention does not alter present-day space directly,” Kael said, thinking aloud. “It preserves an old relationship between objects and compels processes to treat that relationship as valid.”

Drenched beside him, Ollan watched the water.

“Did you just say the road is dead, but the world still respects its opinion?”

Kael paused.

“That formulation is offensively imprecise.”

“So I was close.”

Before Kael could answer, the high hum of a mana drive came from the direction of the farm.

A small cargo cart was travelling along the modern farm road. A young worker sat behind its control frame, and several wooden crates stood on the platform. The vehicle should have passed the cordon, but a few dozen metres away it suddenly jerked and turned into the wheat.

Mirael immediately raised her hand.

“Stop the vehicle!”

The young man was already trying. He pulled the brake lever, then turned the controls back, but the cart continued straight ahead, moved precisely onto the old road’s line, and smoothly followed its curve.

Ollan stopped smiling at once.

“Navigation has taken over the drive. He isn’t making that turn.”

Mirael ran towards the vehicle. Kael unfolded the old map and felt the shape of the route ahead snap into place in his mind. A new shed stood in the old road’s path. Beyond it, the former road led to a bridge that no longer existed. Since the stream had changed course, all that remained there was a steep, muddy ravine.

“It will keep following the old route,” Kael said, then raised his voice. “Ollan, don’t disconnect the main accumulator. We’ll lose the brake booster. Disable the navigation circuit!”

“Already on it!”

Ollan tore an impulse spike from his belt and ran to intercept the cart. Meanwhile, Mirael caught up, seized its side frame, and jumped onto the step. The driver was struggling to hold the controls while shouting something to her through the rain.

“Take your hands off the frame!” Mirael ordered. “If the system thinks it’s steering correctly, you won’t persuade it otherwise.”

Ollan threw the spike. Metal struck the rear compartment, and a blue pulse ran across the body. The cart swerved. At the same moment, Mirael struck the emergency lever with her palm. The wheels locked, the vehicle slid across the wet ground, slammed sideways into a low wooden post, and finally stopped a few metres from the shed.

For a while, everyone simply listened to the rain.

Ilsa reached the driver first and began checking whether he had struck his head. Mirael jumped down and looked at Kael.

“From this moment, the entire site is closed to civilians. Not just the station.”

Kael nodded without argument.

“Agreed. But the navigation unit must be removed now, before its internal log overwrites the last actions.”

“For a moment I thought you were going to surprise me with unconditional agreement today. Fine. Ten minutes, then we close the road completely.”

Ollan, already opening the vehicle’s rear housing, looked up.

“Twelve.”

“Ten.”

“Eleven, and I promise not to touch anything unrelated to navigation.”

Mirael looked at his open toolkit.

“That promise sounds like a confession to a premeditated crime. Ten.”

Within seven minutes, Ollan had stopped swearing at the rain and started swearing exclusively at the diagnostic results.

There had been no external interference with the unit. No injected command, no concealed control channel, no trace of foreign mana. The modern vehicle had independently recognised the vanished road as an existing transport route. Its log preserved dry technical entries: stable surface detected, route correction performed, optimal path selected.

“The cart is four months old,” Ollan said, tapping the instrument casing. “This unit was assembled in the spring. It physically could not have stored data from the old network.”

“Then the data wasn’t inside it.”

“Where was it, then?”

Kael looked at the field.

Longshanks were moving along the vanished road again. Water still skirted the invisible road surface and drained into nonexistent ditches.

“Perhaps we are thinking about information transfer incorrectly. The vehicle did not receive an old map. It entered an environment in which the old route still has functional significance.”

Ilsa finished dressing a small graze on the driver’s arm and joined them.

“Then it isn’t only shape that’s being preserved.”

“Shape isn’t preserved here at all,” Kael replied. “The road does not exist visually, geometrically, or materially.”

“But animals walk along it, water accounts for its slope, and a machine considers it fit for travel. The road itself vanished long ago, but what it existed for remains.”

Kael understood where she was going before the last word.

“Don’t say ‘role.’”

“I was going to say ‘function.’”

“Then continue.”

Ilsa smiled.

“Though I like ‘role’ better.”

“Role implies far too much intention. The road doesn’t want anything.”

“Judging by what happened to the cart, it has quite a forceful personality.”

Ollan closed the unit’s cover.

“I propose we officially classify it as an evil road.”

Kael wearily ran a hand through his wet hair.

“One day I’ll compile a separate glossary of terms forbidden in my presence.”

“Will the title run to forty pages?”

“The introduction will run to forty pages.”

On the way back to the station, they encountered a Triunity military convoy. Its white-and-silver standards were visible from afar, protective light trembling above the heavy mana tractors. The column was bound for the northern front and had stopped only because the Institute’s new signal posts partly occupied the junction.

An officer stepped out of the first vehicle and almost immediately demanded they clear the way. Mirael explained that the main highway remained open, but turning into the fields was temporarily prohibited because of an active anomaly.

“What level?” he asked.

“Category Three observation for now. Today we obtained the first confirmed effect on a civilian machine.”

The officer looked at the wet cart beside the shed and frowned slightly.

“Has the report been sent to ecclesiastical oversight?”

“Central Administration receives copies automatically. What it passes on to our allies is outside my remit.”

“Since the recent incidents, everything serious involving the Underside goes through the Aerind observers. If you confirm an archē anomaly, they’ll send a representative anyway.”

Kael committed the name to memory.

Aerind.

He had heard of them in connection with the Triunity, though he had never encountered them personally. Elgarion’s alliance with the ecclesiastical state was primarily geopolitical. The Triunity occupied an enormous stretch of territory between Elgarion and the advancing domains of Malferium, so much of the western frontier enjoyed relative peace precisely because someone else was fighting a war. Elgarion supplied technology, specialists, and intelligence in return. No serious person called it friendship.

Meanwhile, the officer pointed at Kael’s instruments.

“What exactly have you found?”

“A phenomenon that preserves the functional relationships of destroyed infrastructure after its physical elements have disappeared. We are using the preliminary designation ‘retention effect.’”

“I see. Another hole in the world, then.”

Kael frowned.

“Not a hole. We haven’t confirmed any breach of spatial integrity at all.”

The officer looked at him for a few seconds, then at Ollan.

“Is he always like this?”

“You’re seeing him after breakfast. This is him in good condition.”

Mirael intervened before the conversation lost all professional value. A few minutes later, the column moved on. Its heavy rumble gradually faded beyond the hills, and Kael watched it go for a while.

Ilsa noticed.

“Don’t like the Triunity getting the report?”

“I’m more interested in why their observers are already collecting cases like this. If the church has established a dedicated branch for archē anomalies, there must be enough of them that they are no longer considered exclusively the Institute’s problem.”

“Or they simply don’t trust us.”

“Those explanations are not mutually exclusive.”

Kael spent the rest of the day testing his new hypothesis. If what persisted was the functional connection between elements of the old system, then a modern object placed within that structure should somehow take it into account.

Ollan brought two identical portable mana markers. Under normal conditions, each sent a short energy pulse to the nearest configured receiver. They placed one directly on the former road and the other several metres away. The receiver stood ahead of them, at almost the same distance from both.

The first marker worked normally. A blue mana line flashed towards the receiver and vanished.

The second sent a pulse that first went forward, then turned sharply left and disappeared into an empty patch of field.

Ollan stared for several seconds at the place where the energy had gone.

“If it turns out there’s an old pylon buried there, I’m going to be very annoyed.”

Taven had already unfolded the archival map.

“Not buried. Dismantled fourteen years ago.”

Kael moved closer. On the old plan, a distribution node connecting the road to the station stood in that exact spot.

Ollan activated the marker again.

The pulse followed the same route and broke off at the point where no receiver existed any longer.

That was the moment when their previous definition of Retention became irretrievably too narrow.

It was not only the state of a ruined circuit that persisted. Not only a tower’s shadow. Not even the road as a former stretch of space. The old system continued to recognise its own connections. A new mechanism, built many years later and never connected to the vanished network, had been placed within its former structure and begun behaving as though the old nodes still had the right to participate in mana transmission.

Kael slowly traced a finger over the archival map.

The station connected to the tower. The tower to the road. The road to the distribution pylon. Beyond that, the network continued towards the woods.

“We decided too soon that the anomaly was at the station,” he said. “The station was merely the first place where the effect was noticeable enough for someone to file a request.”

Mirael stepped closer.

“You think the entire old network is being retained?”

“At least some of its relationships. I’m not comfortable with ‘entire’ yet. We don’t know the boundaries, or which historical state has become the basis.”

Taven laid the modern map over the archival one and began following the line with a finger.

“Then we need to look here.”

Beyond the farmland, the old network ran into the woods. A small rectangle on the map was labelled ‘Node 7.’

Beside it was a note: ‘Closed before general modernisation.’

Kael frowned.

“If it was closed before general modernisation, why is it still part of the plan?”

“Because when this map was made, the facility was still included in the distribution structure, even if it wasn’t being used,” Taven answered. “The problem comes later.”

He produced a newer map.

Node 7 was entirely absent.

Not crossed out.

Not marked as dismantled.

It had simply disappeared.

Ollan took the sheet and studied both versions carefully.

“Perhaps the archivists decided a nonfunctional node wasn’t worth transferring to the new plan.”

“Then there should be decommissioning documentation,” Kael said. “Elgarion has paperwork even for dismantling village accumulators. The disappearance of an entire node without a record looks worse than a record of something unpleasant.”

Mirael looked towards the wooded hills ahead.

“We’re not going there today. After the incident with the cart, I’m sending an updated report and requesting permission to expand the zone. We’ll decide how to proceed in the morning.”

Kael wanted to object. Not because the decision was wrong: quite the opposite, it was too sensible to challenge professionally. But the thought that beyond the woods lay the one element of the network that had disappeared from the documents before all the others had already claimed an unpleasantly large share of his attention.

“Very well,” he said at last. “But the report must specifically note that Retention affects connections between elements of a system and can influence modern mechanisms without direct mana contact.”

Ollan looked at Mirael.

“See? He can agree. The sentence just has to be long enough.”

They returned to the inn after dark. The rain had grown heavier again and rustled steadily on the roof while Kael sat by the window with two maps of the Estel site spread before him.

The modern and old versions of the terrain looked like two people who had argued at length about what had happened and now decided to submit written testimony.

One sheet showed a field, a new highway, an altered stream, and farm buildings.

The other showed a road, a tower, a distribution pylon, an old station, and Node 7.

Kael opened his notebook. Yesterday’s definition, ‘retention of a preceding state,’ now looked almost naive. He did not cross it out. Incorrect hypotheses had value too, if they honestly showed how little data a person had needed to be wrong.

Below it, he wrote:

*“The retention effect can preserve functional and spatial relationships after the physical disappearance of the elements that formed those relationships.”*

He examined the sentence, considered it briefly, then added:

*“Working hypothesis: what is retained is not an individual thing, but a system of connections.”*

He liked that last formulation considerably less.

Too broad.

If it proved correct, they would have to establish where the system ended.

The station was a system.

So was the regional network.

The road connected settlements.

People connected the settlements.

Beyond that, the word ‘system’ began expanding so rapidly that, for the first time, professional terminology became not a way of organising the problem but a way of seeing its potential size.

Ollan’s voice came from the next room:

“Still hunched over that map?”

“Working.”

“That wasn’t a question about your occupation. I’m asking whether you intend to sleep tonight.”

Kael looked at the line leading to Node 7.

“When I finish.”

“You never finish.”

“Not true. Sometimes the subject of research comes to an end.”

There was silence behind the wall for several seconds.

“After yesterday, that’s a very bold formulation.”

Kael smiled despite himself.

Perhaps Ollan occasionally did contribute something useful to terminology.

He looked down at his personal list and added two new entries.

*“40. Reason for the continued connectivity of the Estel system after the destruction of most of its physical components.”*

*“41. Node 7.”*

There he stopped.

Rain continued tapping against the glass. Somewhere beyond Estel, water was gathering again in ditches that did not exist. Young longshanks walked a road they had never seen. New mechanisms found old nodes that had vanished long before those mechanisms were built.

Kael was still unwilling to call it memory. Memory implied someone who remembered, and none of the facts he knew entitled him to attribute consciousness to the world.

But one thing was becoming harder and harder to deny.

Something at the Estel site was not preserving things.

It was preserving the relationships between them.

And if the old network truly continued to exist in some state corresponding neither to matter nor to the modern map, then tomorrow they would have to go to the place where that network broke off on paper.

Kael closed his notebook.

Node 7 had disappeared from the documents many years ago.

All that remained was to discover whether it had agreed to disappear from the world.
