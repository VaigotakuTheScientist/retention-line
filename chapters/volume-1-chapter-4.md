The Institute began taking Estel apart before anyone had decided whether to call the situation a state of emergency.

By eight in the morning, temporary barriers stood at the western junction, thin Archē Isolation signal posts stretched across the fields, and the old station had acquired a second cordon and two district officials whose principal task was to prevent curious residents from proving their own immunity to warning signs. The energy service disconnected three reserve branches running near the former network. The municipal archive was forbidden to issue technical maps of the Estel site without the Institute’s approval. That last measure provoked more resentment than all the others combined.

Estel’s administrator, Arden Voss, came to the inn in person.

He was a short man with neatly trimmed grey temples, a dark frock coat, and the expression of someone who preferred to solve problems before they turned into public meetings. Judging by how tightly he gripped his folder of complaints, this time the problems had got there first.

“You’ve closed two farm roads, restricted access to the northern sector, and now you’re forbidding the archive to issue maps,” he told Mirael. “I’m trying to explain to the local farms why they can’t transport their harvest by the usual route, and so far the best official answer amounts to ‘the road occasionally thinks it still exists.’”

Seated at the next table, Ollan raised a finger.

“If I may, that’s already considerably more precise than the original descriptions.”

Kael looked at him.

“No one asked you.”

“I’m assisting the state administration.”

Arden Voss looked between them and evidently decided not to establish which of the two young specialists posed a greater danger to the administrative process.

Mirael remained calm.

“The fields will reopen as soon as we confirm that the modern infrastructure no longer interacts with the old network. Yesterday, a civilian cart lost control. Today, we know the object can incorporate new mechanisms into connections that existed before they were built. Until we understand the boundary of that influence, I will not authorise traffic through the zone.”

“I understand the risk. I need an estimate of the timeframe.”

“I don’t have one.”

“An approximate one, at least.”

“If I say three days, and in three days we discover the road has restored another half of the old network, will it help you that I was confident for the first two?”

Arden looked at her for several seconds, then let out a weary breath.

“No. But it will help me explain to people that at least you aren’t pretending.”

He opened the folder and placed another document on the table.

“Then the next problem. The district archive refuses to comply fully with your order.”

Kael raised his head.

“On what grounds?”

“Very Elgarian ones. The archivists maintain that if the Institute prohibits access to a document because the act of reading it may itself be dangerous, the order must include an information-hazard classification. Yours does not.”

Ollan laughed softly.

“I love this country.”

Kael took the document.

“Technically, they’re right.”

Mirael looked at him.

“Don’t even start.”

“I’m not disputing the decision. I’m saying that the existing protocols really do address dangerous objects, rather than danger from information about those objects.”

“Then after this case, the Collegium will receive a new appendix to the regulations.”

“If the case ends.”

The room became quieter for a moment.

No one commented on that last sentence.

After Node 7, the word ‘ends’ had acquired an unpleasant ambiguity.

Arden Voss gathered some of the documents and was about to leave when the inn’s door opened again.

Noren Schal stood on the threshold.

Kael did not immediately understand what was wrong with him. The caretaker wore the same old energy-service jacket as on the day they met, his hair stuck out in its familiar disorder, and he held a rolled-up work cap. But he looked as though he had been awake all night and spent most of it trying to convince himself he had been dreaming.

“Mr Schal?” Mirael’s tone changed at once. “What happened?”

Noren looked at her, then at Kael.

“Your door was in the wrong place yesterday.”

Kael felt his attention gather in its familiar way.

“Which door?”

“At the station. The inner one.”

“There’s only one.”

Noren nodded slowly.

“Now there is.”

Arden Voss stopped gathering papers.

Ollan set down his mug.

Kael indicated an empty chair.

“Sit down and tell us from the beginning. Don’t try to select what sounds reasonable.”

“Then we’ll be here a while.”

“Good.”

Noren sat.

For the first few minutes, his account really did sound like the aftermath of a bad dream. He remembered a second service door in the station’s northern wall, which did not exist in the present room. Beyond it, supposedly, lay a narrow staircase leading down, then an emergency cabinet marked in green and an old side channel where mechanics checked the mana line’s pressure. Noren could describe the cabinet handles’ positions, the oil used for the relay, even the curse favoured by a foreman who constantly struck his head on a low crossbeam.

The problem was that Noren had never worked there.

At least, that was what his documents—and Noren himself as recently as yesterday—claimed.

“I was on the eastern crew,” he said, squeezing his cap. “Then they transferred me closer to Estel, when the old line was hardly used any more. I only knew this station from outside. And last night I woke up able to walk through it with my eyes shut.”

“Did you see it in a dream?” Ilsa asked.

“At first, yes. Then I went to wash my face and remembered where the cabinet’s spare key was kept.”

“Remembered, or guessed?”

Noren gave her an irritated look.

“I know the difference between ‘I think’ and ‘I remember.’ That’s why I came.”

Ilsa took no offence.

“I need to ask unpleasant questions. If a memory appeared from outside, detail does not prove it true.”

Kael nodded.

“A person can remember something that never happened with complete confidence. The brain doesn’t attach a state seal of authenticity to a memory simply because it’s detailed.”

Noren smiled without amusement.

“You certainly know how to reassure people.”

“That is not the purpose of diagnosis.”

“Kael,” Mirael said quietly.

“I know.”

Ilsa took Noren into the next room and spent almost an hour checking everything she could without complex equipment. Pulse, responses, mana circulation, nervous system, residual influences on the soul. Noren proved remarkably healthy for a man his age, apart from sleep deprivation and slightly elevated blood pressure.

He had no Signature.

That mattered.

A Signature arose at birth only in some people, when a small intervention of archē left a stable pattern in the forming soul. In some, that trace manifested hardly at all for decades; in others, it later became the direction of an Ability after Awakening. Noren belonged to the majority whose souls had never displayed such a structure. He was not Awakened, possessed no rare sensitivity to archē, and had no obvious reason to be particularly susceptible to the Underside.

Retention had not selected him for being unusual.

That was worse.

“No foreign soul circuit,” Ilsa said when they returned to the common room. “Normal mana circulation. No signs of possession. No influence I can distinguish from his own state.”

Ollan folded his arms.

“So either he really remembered something, or he’s found a very elaborate way to ruin our morning.”

Noren looked up.

“I’m sixty-one. At my age, joints are enough for that.”

Even Mirael smiled briefly.

Kael placed a blank sheet in front of Noren.

“Draw the station as you remember it. Don’t look at our maps. If you’re uncertain about a detail, mark the uncertainty separately. Don’t try to make the drawing pretty.”

Noren picked up the pencil.

“That requirement I can meet without difficulty.”

Fifteen minutes later, a plan had appeared on the sheet.

Uneven, not to scale, but clear enough.

The main room.

The distribution panel.

The inner door.

A second door to the north.

Stairs.

An emergency cabinet.

A side service channel.

Taven laid several archival copies beside it and began comparing them.

At first, he said nothing.

Then he pointed to one of the oldest plans.

“The second door is there.”

Ollan leaned closer.

“Date?”

“Twenty-one years ago.”

“So, several years before the configuration that manifested in the circuit.”

Kael looked at Noren.

“Have you ever seen this plan?”

“If I had, I wouldn’t be sitting here with this look on my face.”

Taven continued comparing.

“The emergency cabinet matches. So do the stairs. But there’s no side channel here.”

“On the others?”

Taven examined two more sheets.

“No.”

Kael felt the familiar surge of interest, immediately suppressed by a more unpleasant thought.

If one portion of the memories was confirmed by documents while another was absent even from them, there were two possibilities. Noren had added an invented detail to genuine memories: perfectly ordinary memory behaviour. Or he had preserved information about a configuration the archive no longer contained.

The second possibility required testing.

Mirael agreed to return to the station only after the district detachment had completely cleared the room and reinforced the cordon. They took Noren with them, though he asked several times whether it would not be simpler just to declare him an old man with bad dreams.

“It would,” Kael replied. “For now, the facts are getting in the way.”

“An inconvenient profession you have.”

“Yes.”

The station greeted them with silence.

The ruined circuit still hung on the far wall. Ollan’s fresh scratch remained in place, the old inner door stood open, and under the midday sun there were no shadows of nonexistent towers to be seen.

Noren stopped in the centre.

“Here.”

He pointed at the northern wall.

Stone masonry.

No trace of a door.

Taven laid out four markers and activated Reference Grid. Space settled into a strict system of measurements in his perception.

“Ordinary wall. No room behind it in the present geometry.”

“And according to the old plan?” Kael asked.

“There should be a narrow corridor.”

Kael turned to Noren.

“Don’t try to see the door. Close your eyes and remember going through it.”

Noren frowned.

“I’m still not sure I ever did.”

“Certainty about your biography isn’t what matters. Remember the feeling itself.”

Noren closed his eyes.

For several seconds, nothing happened.

Then Taven raised his head.

“There’s a deviation.”

Mirael responded at once:

“What kind?”

“The wall’s depth has changed.”

“By how much?”

“Seven centimetres. No… eight.”

Noren opened his eyes.

Reference Grid returned to its original values.

For several seconds, no one spoke.

Kael moved closer to the wall.

“Again. This time, remember the emergency cabinet.”

Noren concentrated.

Ollan’s manometric detector clicked softly.

A brief mana response appeared where no cabinet existed.

Not strong.

Not even stable enough to call an active circuit.

But it appeared.

Noren stopped remembering, and the response disappeared.

Ollan looked at the instrument.

“I don’t like this.”

“Very informative,” Kael observed.

“Fine. I don’t like memory-confirmed temporary restoration of the parameters of an absent object in the absence of an energy carrier. Happy?”

Kael considered.

“Much better.”

Ilsa was watching Noren, not the instruments.

“Let’s test words separately. Mr Schal, say ‘emergency cabinet’ without picturing it.”

“Emergency cabinet.”

Nothing.

“Now remember the handle.”

Noren frowned.

The instrument responded again.

That was when Kael understood the crucial distinction.

Information by itself was not sufficient.

Speaking the name did nothing.

Simply knowing probably did nothing either.

What mattered was the internal act of acknowledging the past state as something experienced.

“Testimony,” Ilsa said quietly.

Kael looked at her.

“What?”

“The map testifies that the road existed. The register testifies that the object belonged to the system. And Noren testifies that the cabinet stood here. Only not with words. With memory itself.”

“The term is too legalistic.”

“And yours?”

Kael was about to answer with something like ‘retention-induced restoration of functional binding through an internally corroborated mnemonic structure,’ but stopped.

Ollan saw his expression.

“Please don’t say it.”

Kael looked at Noren, then at the wall.

“Testimony is acceptable for now.”

Ilsa smiled.

“A historic moment. Kael has voluntarily accepted a term shorter than six words.”

“I said ‘for now.’”

It was the only light moment for the next several hours.

Back at the inn, Kael requested not only documents concerning Node 7 but lists of the personnel involved in closing the old network. The reply arrived towards evening and at first seemed entirely ordinary.

Nine specialists.

Engineers.

Two mana technicians.

A cartographer.

An employee of municipal oversight.

One representative of the old Academic Commission on Anomalies, a body that had existed long before the present Institute of Reverse Circuitry was formed.

After Node 7 closed, the group disbanded.

Kael began with subsequent appointments.

The first engineer left for the northern district.

The second took a position at the academy.

The third was transferred west.

A year later, the cartographer moved to the roads administration.

Two left the service early.

Another specialist took an administrative post entirely unrelated to mana networks.

Ollan looked over his shoulder.

“An ordinary reorganisation after the upgrade.”

“Possibly.”

Kael kept searching.

None of the nine had received disciplinary sanctions.

Four had been promoted.

Two had received state payments for work at a difficult facility.

In other words, they had not been punished.

Kael opened the joint assignment register.

After the Node’s closure, none of them had ever taken part in an operation with any of the others.

Ever.

He stopped turning pages.

Mirael noticed first.

“What did you find?”

Kael did not answer immediately. He took a clean sheet and wrote down the nine names. Beside them, the cities and departments to which they had been sent.

The lines spread across the whole western part of Elgarion.

Not randomly.

Too evenly.

“This wasn’t a disbandment.”

Ollan moved closer.

“What was it, then?”

“If the aim had simply been to close the team down, they could have assigned people to neighbouring departments. Here, each person ended up in a separate professional environment. Even specialists in the same field never crossed paths again.”

“You’re saying they were deliberately kept apart?”

Kael picked up a pencil and drew the old working connections between the names, then crossed them out.

“They were decomposed.”

Ollan sighed.

“In human language.”

Kael looked at the list.

This time without irritation.

“They were arranged so they could never form their old group again.”

The room fell silent.

Ilsa was the first to look at Noren, who sat by the window drinking tea.

Mirael looked at the old documents.

Kael felt the next thought forming of its own accord.

An unpleasant one.

So logical that he wanted to find a flaw in it.

He looked at his own team.

Mirael.

Ollan.

Ilsa.

Taven.

Five people who had entered Node 7 together.

Five people who shared the same knowledge of its internal layout, the warning on the wall, the old network’s behaviour, the way the door appeared, the link between the map and activation.

Five witnesses to one system.

“Mirael.”

She had already understood where the question was going.

“Go on.”

“If, after the operation, the Collegium determines that the group’s shared memory sustains retention connectivity, which protocol will they apply?”

Ollan looked from one to the other.

“Wait. What do you mean, ‘shared memory sustains’?”

Mirael did not answer him straight away.

“It depends on the level of evidence.”

“Assume the connection is confirmed.”

She looked at the nine names.

“Then they’ll separate us.”

Ollan gave an almost automatic smile.

“For quarantine?”

Mirael said nothing.

The smile disappeared.

Ilsa asked:

“For how long?”

“If they establish that sustained contact between the people carrying the memories can itself restore the structure… until the risk is gone.”

“Meaning?”

Mirael looked up.

“Possibly forever.”

No one spoke.

Outside the window, Estel was having an ordinary evening. A woman with a basket walked down the street. Someone brought a wooden board out of the bakery opposite. Farther away, someone was arguing about the closed road.

Ollan broke the silence first.

“A very inconvenient job.”

Kael looked at the old team.

No punishments.

No prisons.

Just nine people who, one day, were no longer allowed to be nine people together.

And the worst part was that Kael could not immediately call that decision wrong.

If the group’s presence really did sustain the anomaly, the Collegium was obliged to break the connection.

For the first time, he faced an order no one had yet given, but to which he could already find no professional objection.

“It’s still a hypothesis,” Ilsa said.

“Yes,” Kael agreed. “And I very much hope it stays that way.”

Noren spoke unexpectedly:

“I remembered someone.”

They all looked at him.

“Who?”

He set down his cup.

“I don’t know his name. The man in charge of the station. Or not the station… the whole branch. Tall, already grey then. He made us take down the signs.”

Kael approached slowly.

“‘Us’?”

Noren went still.

He had heard it himself.

“I…”

Ilsa immediately moved closer.

“Take your time.”

“I was there.”

He was not looking at them.

Somewhere past them.

“Not as a caretaker. Before that. Very young. They sent me from the eastern team for a month. They were falling behind with dismantling the line.”

Kael opened Noren’s employment records.

His official biography contained nothing of the kind.

But if the assignment had been brief…

He requested the old payrolls.

They had to wait almost forty minutes.

A line appeared in one of them.

“N. Schal. Temporary technical assignment. Branch 7. 31 days.”

Noren looked at the entry as though it belonged to someone else.

“Why didn’t I remember this?”

No one answered.

Because the question had now changed.

Not where the memory had come from.

But why it had disappeared.

Noren began remembering faster.

Metal dust on his hands.

Old signs packed into crates and taken away separately from the equipment.

An engineer who made them rewrite the routes.

People forbidden to use the old node numbers even in conversation.

“We didn’t just change the maps,” he said. “They made us walk along the new road.”

Kael looked up.

“What?”

“When they built it, everyone still took the shortcut along the old road for the first few weeks. He said we couldn’t do that. You can’t make people forget an old road by issuing an order. You have to give them a new one and walk it long enough for it to become the only one.”

Kael felt a chill run down his spine.

The people of the past had not understood Retention in modern terms.

Perhaps they had not even known the word.

But they had understood the principle.

Not to destroy the old.

To create a new state strong enough for the old one to lose its connections.

“Who said that?”

Noren closed his eyes.

“Venn.”

Kael froze.

“His first name?”

“I don’t remember. Master Venn. Everyone called him that.”

Taven was already searching the old lists.

A few minutes later, he found him.

Loer Venn.

An employee of the Academic Commission for Spatial and Archē Deviations.

One of those involved in closing Node 7.

Alive.

Eighty-two years old.

After the operation, he had left fieldwork and never worked on mana networks again.

Kael looked at the address.

There it was.

A direction.

Not the chance appearance of another witness, but a man whom Node 7 itself had connected to the investigation through Noren.

He was about to dictate a request to make contact when Taven said:

“The map.”

One word.

His tone did the rest.

A modern map of the Estel district lay on the table.

Kael had seen it that morning.

The old road had not been on it.

Now a thin grey line crossed the field.

At first he thought it was a pencil mark.

He ran a finger over it.

Ink.

The same printing as the rest of the map.

Noren was looking at it.

“There was a fork there.”

A short side branch slowly emerged on the map.

It did not flash.

It did not appear all at once.

The thin line seemed to be acquiring the right to be printed.

Mirael said sharply:

“Master Schal, don’t remember anything else.”

Noren looked at her.

“I’m not doing it on purpose.”

“That’s precisely why you must stop.”

Kael pulled the map closer.

“Let’s test it.”

Ilsa frowned.

“Carefully.”

“Just one memory.”

He turned to Noren.

“Say the word ‘station’.”

“Station.”

Nothing.

“Say ‘emergency cabinet’.”

Noren repeated it.

The map did not change.

“Now remember it.”

Noren hesitated.

“Kael…”

“One detail. The handle.”

He closed his eyes.

A tiny rectangular mark appeared beside the old station.

Kael stopped the experiment at once.

“That’s enough.”

Noren opened his eyes.

The mark stopped growing.

Mirael was looking at Kael.

“Your conclusion?”

He did not answer immediately.

Because he disliked the formulation the facts now demanded.

“Testimony works both ways.”

Ollan frowned.

“Explain.”

“Until now, we’ve seen old records sustaining an old system. Now Noren’s internally restored memory is forcing a modern description to include elements of the past. The map doesn’t merely help Retention preserve the old state.”

He looked at the grey line.

“Retention is beginning to bring the modern description into line with the testimony.”

Ilsa said quietly:

“So the more he remembers…”

“The more grounds the world has for considering the old state admissible.”

Noren went pale.

“Am I dangerous now?”

No one answered immediately.

And that was an answer in itself.

Ilsa was the first to return to professional reasoning.

“We can limit the stimuli. Isolate him from maps, the station, old objects. If associations trigger the memories, we remove the associations.”

“They started at night,” Kael said.

“Then sedation.”

“Sleep was the first state in which the process accelerated.”

“Not necessarily every kind of sleep. Medical sedation may suppress the activity.”

“May.”

“Kael.”

“I’m not rejecting the option. I’m listing the uncertainties.”

Mirael raised a hand.

“No hasty decisions. Noren stays here under observation. Ilsa will prepare the mildest regimen to suppress excitation without deep sleep. Taven, remove all the maps. Ollan, switch off any instruments that might record changes automatically. Kael, with me.”

They went into the next room.

Mirael closed the door.

“You already think of him as a node.”

The wording struck closer than Kael had expected.

“I think of him as an active carrier of retention testimony.”

“That’s exactly what I said, only in a way that remembers we’re talking about a person.”

Kael said nothing.

“What’s happening?”

He began laying out the chain.

“Noren recovers a memory. Retention gains additional testimony. The modern map changes. The more old elements the system recognises again, the greater the retention pressure around him. That probably makes further recovery of memory easier.”

“Positive feedback.”

“Yes. Remembering strengthens the conditions for the next act of remembering.”

“Can we break it?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s an important part.”

“I know.”

Mirael looked at him for a long time.

“Then don’t make the decision for everyone before it becomes necessary.”

“I don’t intend to.”

That was true.

At that moment.

They returned to the others.

Noren sat quietly. Ilsa had given him a mild sedative that did not induce sleep. The maps had been put in a metal container. Taven had kept only blank sheets for recording current changes.

The next hour passed without new memories.

Then Noren asked for water.

Ten minutes later, he said:

“I remembered the foreman’s surname.”

Ilsa gently stopped him.

“Don’t say it.”

“But I’ve already remembered.”

A short line emerged on one of the blank sheets beside the archive container.

Not a map.

Ordinary paper.

Taven noticed first.

Kael felt something slowly sink inside his chest.

They had removed the maps.

Retention had found another medium for a description.

Not the form of the document.

The very fact that a system of observation existed around Noren.

Mirael ordered the paper removed as well.

Ollan switched off every instrument.

Taven withdrew his Grid.

But a few minutes later, a thin scratch appeared on the wooden tabletop, tracing the old branch.

“It uses the nearest available description,” Kael said.

Ilsa looked at him.

“Then we ourselves are the nearest available description.”

And that was when he understood why the old specialists had been sent to different parts of the country.

Not just maps.

Not just people individually.

A group, as collective testimony, could be more stable than one person.

They had been separated because, together, they remembered the same system too well.

Kael looked at Mirael.

She understood without words.

So did Ollan.

“Damn it,” he said quietly.

Noren looked between them.

“What?”

Kael wanted to answer, but Noren suddenly straightened.

“There was another route.”

Mirael said:

“Don’t go on.”

“No, wait. This is important. We closed it last.”

A new line appeared on the table.

It led from the place where Node 7 should have been.

Kael stopped hearing the room as a whole.

Not literally.

Everything else simply receded.

The line.

The warning on the metal wall.

“If the Node begins to be counted as the seventh again—do not look for the eighth.”

At the time, he had taken it as a warning against a search.

An expedition.

An investigation.

Now he understood his mistake.

You did not necessarily have to seek the eighth on foot.

Restoring it to the system was enough.

Noren continued:

“We went there at night. There were…”

“Master Schal, stop,” Kael said.

Noren looked at him, but his gaze was no longer here.

“I can see the road. The old… were still standing there…”

The line on the table extended farther.

Taven abruptly activated a minimal Reference Grid.

“Space is reacting.”

“How much?”

“Centimetres, for now. But the direction matches.”

Mirael stepped towards Noren.

“Ilsa, strengthen the sedation circuit.”

“Doing it.”

Ilsa touched his shoulder.

Noren flinched.

A second mark appeared on the table.

Kael saw the new connection instantly.

Ilsa—Noren.

Compensation—stabilisation of his condition.

Stability—the ability to keep remembering.

Even help was becoming part of the system.

“Take your hand away,” he said.

Ilsa looked at him.

“He’s overstimulated.”

“Take it away. Now.”

His tone made her obey.

Noren drew a breath.

“There was a platform at the end. And a sign. I remember the sign.”

The line had almost reached the edge of the table.

Taven went pale.

“The geometry is shifting with it.”

Mirael spoke sharply:

“Noren, look at me. Don’t remember the place. Tell me today’s date. Where are you?”

“Estel. I…”

He blinked.

For a second, he came back.

Kael felt the tension ease.

Then Noren looked towards the old station outside the window.

And something caught hold of the memory again.

“We called it…”

Kael understood everything at once.

There was no time to wait and see whether the next dose would work.

He could not use Coupling: the attempt to read would itself create a new dependency.

They could not stabilise Noren with mana.

They could not let him fall asleep.

There was no known way to erase a memory instantly without damaging the soul.

And above all—they already knew what would happen if he finished.

The line’s endpoint was forming on the table.

The eighth.

Kael did not remember drawing his field knife.

An ordinary Institute tool. It was used to cut cable sheathing, take bark samples and open reagent packages.

Noren said:

“Eigh…”

Kael was beside him before anyone understood the movement.

With one hand, he caught him by the shoulder from behind.

With the other, he drove the knife into the side of his neck in one short thrust.

His speech broke off.

Noren jerked.

A drop of blood fell onto the table.

The line stopped appearing.

It did not slow.

It stopped.

For several seconds, there was absolute silence in the room.

Then Ilsa cried out:

“Kael?!”

She lunged forwards.

He sharply raised his free hand.

“Don’t touch him!”

“He’s alive!”

“That’s precisely why you mustn’t touch him!”

“You stabbed him!”

“If you restore his circulation and he regains consciousness, the process will continue!”

Ilsa froze.

There was something worse than fury on her face.

Professional understanding.

She had already seen the halted line herself.

Noren sank heavily to the floor.

Mirael was the first to return fully to action.

She wrenched the knife from Kael’s hand and stepped back.

“No one touches the body. Taven, measurements. Ollan, no active circuits. Ilsa—assess his condition without mana.”

“He’s dying.”

Ilsa’s voice shook.

“I asked for his condition.”

She clenched her teeth and crouched beside him without touching him.

“Almost unconscious. Critical blood loss. Without intervention…”

She fell silent.

No one needed the rest.

Taven stood over the table.

The Reference Grid trembled in thin lines.

“Changes have stopped.”

Three words.

Kael felt them pass through the room.

Not a justification.

Confirmation of the mechanism.

And that only made it worse.

Ollan finally looked at Kael.

“What the fuck have you done?”

Kael heard the question.

Almost as a technical one.

And answered it that way.

“Noren was restoring the eighth object. Every memory expanded the old system and increased the likelihood of the next. The map and space had already begun accepting the new branch. The Node’s warning explicitly linked restoration to the eighth. The available suppression methods could not guarantee that memory would cease. Mana techniques created additional connections. If he had finished…”

“I didn’t ask for the calculations!”

For the first time, Ollan was truly shouting at him.

Kael looked at him.

“And for now, I can’t give you any other answer.”

Mirael stood between them.

“Enough. Kael, a full explanation. From the moment he began remembering the new branch.”

He repeated it.

Slowly.

Without trying to soften anything.

Positive feedback.

Changes to modern information media.

A spatial reaction.

The warning about the Eighth.

No reliable way to stop active recollection.

The knife as the only instantaneous, non-magical intervention that did not introduce a complex mana connection.

By the time he finished, Noren was no longer moving.

Ilsa sat beside him.

Not healing him.

Unable to allow herself to heal him.

That was the worst of it.

Mirael asked:

“Did you know death would stop Retention?”

“No.”

Ollan turned sharply.

Kael continued:

“I knew that ending active consciousness had the greatest probability of stopping the process. And that we had no time to test safe alternatives.”

“So you weren’t certain.”

“No.”

He looked at Noren.

For the first time in his entire explanation, his voice changed slightly.

“If I was wrong, I just killed an innocent man.”

No one said he had not been wrong.

That mattered.

Mirael took out her service transmitter.

“From this moment, the room is sealed. No handwritten records until we decide which medium is safe. No one discusses the Eighth object. Even among ourselves. If anyone experiences a new memory, an image, a sense of direction or an urge to draw something in—report it to me without putting its content into words.”

Ollan said dryly:

“A very convenient day for new rules.”

Mirael looked at him.

“A very inconvenient day for the old ones.”

The Central Collegium replied faster than Kael expected.

Mirael reported the death, the observed cessation of anomalous expansion, and the preliminary conclusion that human memory served as retention testimony. The transmitter remained silent for several minutes.

Then the answer arrived.

Not an order to arrest Kael.

Even he was surprised by that.

“Confirm complete isolation of N. Schal’s body. Do not apply restorative or resuscitation techniques until further orders.”

Ilsa read the line and turned away.

The next:

“Seize all altered information media. Do not copy.”

Then:

“Members of Tessen’s group are prohibited from discussing the content of N. Schal’s final memories with one another until the commission arrives.”

And finally:

“Keep Kael Reven under the field curator’s direct supervision. Defer determination of the act’s legal classification until the causal efficacy of the intervention has been established.”

Ollan read it again.

“So the Collegium’s first question is whether the killing worked.”

Kael said nothing.

He would have done the same.

And that was precisely why the remark was so unpleasant.

A few minutes later, another message arrived.

A representative of the Triunity would join the investigation.

Eira Aerind.

Arrival—the following morning.

“Wonderful,” Ollan said. “On top of a religious anomaly, a map that corrects itself and a dead man, we’re now getting a representative of the state whose system of Gifts you suspect of controlled interference with infants’ souls.”

Kael sat by the wall, staring at his hands.

“I don’t intend to discuss that hypothesis with her.”

“For some reason, I can hear ‘yet’ even when you don’t say it.”

Ilsa stood up.

She did not look at Kael.

“I want to go outside.”

Mirael nodded.

“Taven will go with you.”

“I don’t need an escort.”

“Right now, everyone does.”

Ilsa was about to object, then changed her mind.

After the door closed, Ollan stood motionless for a while longer.

Then he sat down opposite Kael.

“Do you even understand that she’ll think about this every time she heals someone now?”

“Yes.”

“That she could have saved him.”

“Yes.”

“And that you forbade her.”

Kael looked up at him for the first time.

“Yes.”

Ollan wanted to say something else.

He did not.

He simply left.

Mirael stayed.

For several minutes, there was only silence between them.

“Show me your hands,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because you’re trying very hard to pretend you’re still working.”

Kael looked down.

His fingers were not shaking.

Yet.

Noren’s blood was on his sleeve.

He noticed it as though for the first time.

“I made the decision before I had time to formulate it fully.”

“I know.”

“Was it right?”

Mirael did not answer immediately.

“I don’t know.”

He gave the faintest smile.

“An unexpectedly professional answer.”

“Don’t try to hide behind a joke now.”

Kael fell silent.

“I know only two things,” she continued. “First: after his death, the changes stopped. Second: we don’t know whether there was another way that could have worked in time. Both will remain true at once.”

That was worse than a simple accusation.

Kael nodded.

Mirael looked at the table, where the old line had broken off halfway.

“One more thing. Do you understand where this conclusion about memory leads?”

“They’ll disperse us.”

“Possibly.”

“If the Collegium is competent—almost certainly.”

She gave him a displeased look.

“You talk about it as though we’re discussing moving equipment.”

“I haven’t yet found wording that changes the outcome.”

“Sometimes wording isn’t for the outcome.”

Kael said nothing.

That, too, was a thought he would have to understand later.

By evening, Noren’s body had been taken away in an isolated container without mana activators. They did not dismantle the altered table: it was covered with neutral fabric and sealed as a whole. All documents on which the old network had begun to appear were collected separately. The modern map of Estel was placed in a metal case.

The unfinished line remained on it.

It led southeast from Node 7.

There was no endpoint.

No number either.

Only a direction.

Kael saw it once before it was sealed.

That proved enough.

That night, he sat by the inn window again.

For the first time in days, there was not a single map before him.

Only his notebook.

For a long time, he did not open it.

Then he did.

He needed to make a personal record of what had happened. Not an official report—he was still forbidden to keep one independently. Just note the facts for himself, without reproducing the dangerous details.

Kael wrote:

“Active retention testimony.”

He looked at it.

Crossed it out.

“Human carrier of restored systemic memory.”

Crossed that out too.

The formulations were technically acceptable.

And completely wrong.

They turned what had happened into a problem to be solved.

Noren had not been a mechanism of the station.

He had not been an emergency circuit.

He had not been a temporary information carrier.

Kael moved to the next line and wrote only:

“Noren Schal.”

He looked at the name for a long time.

No definition.

No classification.

There was still space on his personal list of things it was too early to give a proper name.

Kael did not add Noren to it.

The man had already been given a name.

The problem was not that Kael had failed to name him.

The problem was how easily, a few hours earlier, he had managed to call him a node.

Beyond the wall of his room, the inn was quiet. Ollan was not arguing with the innkeeper. Ilsa was not talking to Taven. Mirael was working downstairs alone.

They had already begun to be separated.

For now, only by an order not to discuss recent events.

Into separate rooms.

Until morning.

Kael suddenly imagined the same thing happening forever.

Ollan leaving for another district.

Ilsa receiving an appointment to the Somatic Directorate in the north.

Taven returning to Topology.

Mirael never again being assigned an operation with any of them.

Kael himself being sent to Central Archē Analytics, where he could see their names in documents for years without being allowed to write a letter, if the very existence of a lasting connection between them was deemed dangerous.

He tried to find a professional objection.

He found none.

If the group became a system, the system would be dismantled.

The old engineers had already done the same thing once.

Kael finally understood why almost nothing remained of their history.

They had not been forgotten.

They had been made to stop confirming one another.

He closed his eyes.

And then he remembered the line.

Not the map.

Not the endpoint.

Only the direction.

Southeast.

Kael opened his eyes.

For several seconds, he sat completely still.

There it was.

Noren’s final complication.

He had not managed to name the Eighth.

He had not managed to restore the object completely.

But the direction had passed to Kael in time.

Now it existed neither on a map.

Nor in documents.

Inside him.

And if their conclusions were correct, that might already be enough for it one day to become a connection.

Kael opened his notebook and almost wrote a new line.

He stopped.

Remembered the words on the wall.

“Do not look for the eighth.”

He closed the page.

For the first time in his life, deliberately leaving a question unrecorded.

That should have been reassuring.

It was not.

Because Kael Reven had killed Noren Schal before he could remember the Eighth node.

But now Kael remembered the direction to it.
