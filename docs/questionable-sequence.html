A Sequence of Questionable Significance

An independent humorous short story, not a chapter of The Retention Line.

Approximately twelve million years after the disappearance of humanity–or, more precisely, twelve million years after the last historian had stopped arguing about the definition of humanity–Maria and Nushi were exploring the ruins of an ancient educational institution.

There was nothing particularly unusual about this activity. Both were professional investigators of extinct civilizations, and both had accumulated sufficient intellectual achievements to make an ordinary conversation unnecessarily complicated. Their current expedition, however, had presented them with an architectural irregularity of considerable epistemological inconvenience.

Heading down a hallway, Maria noticed a suspicious bridge. According to her preliminary observations, it did not quite fit within the physical dimensions of the building that supposedly contained it.

“Nushi, don’t you find this bridge to be a rather inappropriate existence in space?”

“Well, yes. It provokes a glimpse of suspicion from the very first moment of perception.”

They stopped.

This was not because stopping would assist them in understanding the suspicious bridge, but because both considered it academically irresponsible to continue walking before acknowledging that something potentially interesting had happened.

Maria examined the ceiling. It was three meters above the floor, except where it appeared to be underneath it. Through the left window she observed a distant galaxy, despite having entered the building from a perfectly ordinary planet. Through the right window she observed herself observing the left window, an event which had apparently occurred tomorrow.

“Fascinating,” she announced.

“What, specifically?”

“The temporal asymmetry of the observational conditions.”

“Could you elaborate?”​

“I could. But I suspect doing so would add very little information while requiring a statistically significant number of additional words.”

“Then please proceed.”

Maria did proceed, indeed. For several minutes she explained that an event situated in the future could, under certain highly questionable physical assumptions, be observed from the present, provided the observer occupied a region of spacetime in which the future had already happened but had not yet become the past.

Nushi listened with the solemn respect traditionally reserved for statements that contained enough technical vocabulary to discourage interruption.

When Maria finished, Nushi checked the chronometer.

“Four seconds have passed.”

“Subjectively?”

“Objectively.”

“Extraordinary. We have spent more than a hundred words describing four seconds.”

“An impressive achievement. Shall we continue?”

They entered the suspicious bridge, which was either twenty metres long or infinitely long, depending on whether one measured it from the entrance or the exit. Neither had time to investigate this contradiction, largely because neither could agree whether time was currently available.

At its centre stood a sign.

The sign was wooden, square and visibly ancient. It displayed considerable evidence of prolonged, non-consensual exposure to the passage of time, including scratches, cracks and a general reluctance to remain structurally cooperative. Maria estimated its age at seven thousand years. Nushi estimated it at eight thousand. They agreed that the disagreement was not especially important but might become useful if they ever needed to fill another paragraph.

The object resembled a sign from an archaic digital simulation known as Minecraft, although its most remarkable property was not its appearance.

It was the writing.

Maria leaned forward. She had deciphered extinct languages, reconstructed lost mathematical systems and once spent an entire afternoon debating whether an omniscient entity could meaningfully be surprised. Yet she could not detect any coherent relationship between the symbols before her.

“Nushi,” she said, “I believe we have discovered something profoundly disturbing.”

“An existential threat?”

“Potentially, but let’s not hurry to the conclusion. The message written on the sign of the suspicious bridge resembles a list with no organising principle.”

Nushi approached. The expression on Nushi’s face evolved from curiousity to intellectual concern, then to the quiet despair characteristic of a researcher confronted with data that refused to become knowledge.

Maria read the inscription aloud:

“As was explained by the magnificent Thumperfold, Run, Beautiful, Slowly, Table, Quizz, Quick, Think, Thoughtful, Tiger, Blizzard, Gold, Silver, Forest, Mountain, School, Quantum mechanics, AI, Fortnite, Fish, Beef, Pork, Red, Blue, Purple, Hospital, Skyscraper, Gloves, Hoodie, Socks, Terry Crews, Teacher, Buzzcut, Emerald, Ruby.”

Silence followed.

“Perhaps,” Nushi suggested, “this is a compressed account of a historical event.”

“An event involving a tiger, a skyscraper, several gemstones and a man called Terry Crews?”

“History is rarely elegant.”

Maria inspected the bottom of the sign. Beneath the inscription, barely visible, was one final sentence:

English assignment. Use all the words. Minimum 642 words.

For the first time in all the eternal, neither scholar had anything to say.

Nushi slowly turned towards Maria.
