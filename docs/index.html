Volume I

The Present That Never Was

Chapter 1

What Had Already Ended

21 minute read

Kael Reven disliked the word strange.

Not because it sounded bad. Over the long history of the Elgarian language, as far as Kael knew, it had never caused anyone any serious harm, and had even proved somewhat useful to people who wanted to express surprise without burdening themselves with specifics.

That was precisely the problem.

The moment you called a phenomenon strange, the conversation seemed to acquire the legitimate right to end before anyone asked a second question.

Which was why the field directive resting on Kael’s knees irritated him on an almost personal level.

“Strange glow from an old circuit seal.”

He reread the line, hoping that a second acquaintance with it might reveal at least one useful detail.

It did not.

“No duration, no activation frequency, no emission spectrum, no conditions under which it appears,” Kael said. “Even the word old means nothing here. To a district technician, five years is already old. To an archivist, it’s barely had time to dry.”

Across from him, Ollan Veir sat with his head tilted back against the wooden wall of the service carriage, his eyes closed. He possessed the rare ability to imitate sleep so convincingly that most people began to feel embarrassed simply for talking in his vicinity.

Kael had known him far too long to fall for it.

“Add very,” Ollan suggested. “Then it becomes very old. Twice as precise already.”

“That does not increase the precision.”

“It increases the length. Thought you’d appreciate that.”

Ilsa Merr, seated by the opposite window, laughed softly. She held a steaming travel mug in her hands and looked far too pleased for someone about to spend the day beside an abandoned technical facility.

“I’d add presumably very old. Then Kael can argue with the wording all the way to Estel.”

Kael looked at her over the top of the page.

“I am perfectly capable of arguing with it without assistance.”

“That was never in doubt.”

At the front of the carriage, Mirael Tessen leafed through the remaining documents, declining to intervene until the conversation had clearly begun drifting away from work.

She was the senior member of the group and possessed the particular kind of calm that younger specialists often mistook for patience—right up until the first time they made her repeat an order.

Kael had already learned not to make that mistake.

“We’re less than five kilometres from the site,” she said. “Try to preserve at least some of your professional energy until we arrive.”

Kael picked up a pencil and beneath the original description wrote:

Preliminary: spontaneous reactivation of a physically disrupted mana circuit in the absence of any registered power source.

Ollan opened one eye.

“You’ve named a phenomenon you haven’t even seen yet.”

“Working designation.”

“I heard that part. What concerns me isn’t the genre of the name, but the absence of the thing you’re naming.”

“That’s why the designation is preliminary.”

“A wonderfully convenient system. You can be wrong in advance and call it methodology.”

Kael closed the folder.

He would have explained the difference between preliminary classification and error, but at that moment the carriage emerged from the wooded lowlands onto the open road, and his attention shifted outside.

Western Elgarion looked as though the great war existed only on maps and expense sheets.

Low green hills stretched to the horizon. Mana-driven harvesting platforms moved slowly across the fields. Small energy towers stood beside the road, transmitting power between settlements. Somewhere in the distance, a water-pumping station gleamed white.

No smoke.

No fortifications.

No fresh graves beside the road.

War came here differently.

At their previous stop, a shopkeeper had complained about the rising price of mana cells; part of the production was being diverted to the front.

Two hours earlier, their carriage had yielded the road to a column of the Triunity, and for a time the highway had filled with heavy vehicles, people in white-and-silver cloaks, and sealed transport containers.

Then the column disappeared behind the hills, and the road became an ordinary road again.

Kael preferred war in this form.

Tariffs. Delays. Badly completed shipping manifests.

These manifestations of war attempted to kill him considerably less often.

“Counting Triunity vehicles again?” Ilsa asked, noticing where he was looking.

“I was examining the field.”

“Of course.”

“It occupies most of the visible area. Statistically, it is the most probable object of observation.”

Ilsa turned to Ollan.

“Even his excuses sound like reports.”

“That was the short version.”

Mirael did not turn around, but Kael noticed the corner of her mouth twitch.

Their group belonged to the Institute of Reverse Circuitry—one of those Elgarian organisations whose name sounded considerably more important than most of its everyday assignments.

In practice, Institute personnel dealt with places where magical or spatial processes stopped behaving the way they were supposed to.

In one place, a domestic circuit might continue accumulating mana after being disconnected.

Elsewhere, a room might turn out to be several steps longer along one wall than along the other.

Sometimes a mana-beast died, yet the pressure of its aura remained in the same place for several days without any obvious source.

People had long referred to phenomena like these collectively as the Underside of the World.

Kael did not particularly like the term. Too figurative. Too broad.

Still, he had to admit it possessed one virtue.

When an ordinary law suddenly stopped agreeing with its own result, it really did feel as though someone had turned the familiar fabric of reality inside out, exposing the seams.

Those seams were exactly what they looked for.

The station greeted them with a rusted sign.

FACILITY DECOMMISSIONED. ENTRY WITHOUT AUTHORISATION PROHIBITED.

Just below it, someone had added in charcoal:

BUT IT GLOWS.

Kael stopped and reread both inscriptions.

“Unexpectedly, the lower one contains more practical information.”

Ollan jumped down from the carriage and examined the improvised addition.

“Are you suggesting we hand district reporting over to children?”

“If they’re capable of identifying an observable property instead of using the word strange, I’m prepared to consider the reform.”

The building stood apart from the modern road, half overgrown with bushes.

A regional mana line had once passed through here, one of the old systems that supplied several nearby settlements with power for lighting, heating, workshops, and road automation.

After the network was modernised, the line had been moved farther west.

The station itself had been partially dismantled, but the job was never finished. Elgarion, too, observed the ancient engineering principle according to which all demolition ceased the moment the cost of removing the final one percent exceeded the irritation caused by the remaining debris.

A caretaker was waiting for them at the entrance.

Noren Schal was a lean man in his sixties wearing an old district energy-service jacket. His expression suggested that over the previous several days he had been forced to explain the same thing to far too many people.

Mirael showed him the Institute insignia.

“Tessen Group. You submitted the initial report?”

“I did.”

“Tell us everything again. Preferably starting from the first incident.”

Noren looked over the others and sighed heavily.

“The lamp came on.”

Kael looked inside the building.

“What exactly are you referring to as a lamp?”

The caretaker pointed at the far wall.

“The thing that lights up.”

“That is a distribution circuit.”

“When it isn’t glowing, you can call it the hereditary prince for all I care. When it wakes me up through the wall at night, it’s a lamp.”

Kael opened his mouth, but Mirael’s palm settled on his shoulder with the delicate force normally sufficient to terminate terminological wars.

“Continue, Mr Schal.”

Noren had noticed the first activation five nights earlier.

He had heard a relay click inside the old building, assumed a damaged wire had shorted somewhere, gone inside, and seen the circuit glowing.

The light was blue-white and lasted less than a minute.

Then the old protective door activated.

After that, everything went dark.

The subsequent incidents followed roughly the same pattern.

Noren saw the circuit himself twice. On another occasion, two teenagers who had sneaked inside witnessed it. A fourth incident he discovered only through traces left by machinery that had activated.

No one had been injured.

No burns or poisoning had been recorded.

Ilsa immediately took over the questioning.

“After the activations, did you experience headaches, unusual weakness, sleep problems? Perhaps a metallic taste or ringing in your ears?”

“I’m sixty-one, my neighbour owns a rooster, and an old line passes over my house. You want me to separate the symptoms by cause?”

Ilsa considered this for a moment.

“Fair point. Then let’s begin with things you definitely didn’t experience before.”

While she spoke with the caretaker, Taven Ker was already circling the station with a measuring frame.

Taven was officially a spatial cartographer and belonged to the category of people whom others usually noticed only after the first problem occurred.

Tall and thin, with an almost permanently neutral expression, he rarely spoke unless necessary.

Kael had long since concluded that this was not taciturnity.

It was strict conservation of words.

Kael himself went inside with Ollan.

The room smelled of dust, dry wood, and old copper.

Most of the equipment really had been removed: empty cabinets, severed lines, mounting plates on the walls.

The main circuit remained on the far side—a circular system nearly two metres in diameter, embedded in the masonry.

Kael stopped in front of it.

Even without instruments, it was obvious the structure could not function.

Two copper guides were almost entirely missing.

A crack in the wall had severed the third.

The central accumulator had been removed.

Only the mountings remained where the secondary stabiliser had once been.

One junction had melted so long ago that oxidation had turned the edges of the metal into a greenish crust.

Ollan crouched by the base, took out a contact probe, and checked the first line.

“Main channel is broken twice.”

“Three times.”

“Where’s the third?”

Kael pointed higher up.

Ollan stood, inspected the spot, and grimaced.

“Three times. I hate working with you.”

“Because I noticed the break?”

“Because you look pleased whenever you find an additional problem.”

Kael moved closer to the central plate.

“It doesn’t work.”

Ollan raised an eyebrow.

“Finally, a conclusion I can live with.”

“I don’t mean that in the ordinary sense. The structure isn’t merely unpowered. It cannot be powered normally without restoring several physical connections. Even if someone concealed an external source, the energy could not reach most of the circuit.”

Ollan looked at the wall again, and the faint irony disappeared from his face.

“With a bypass channel?”

“You would effectively have to build an entirely new circuit over the old one.”

“Then yes. The ‘lamp’ is getting more interesting.”

Broken things do not work.

Kael did not regard this proposition as a law of the universe.

It was too simple to require a grand theory, which was precisely why it made a good starting point.

If a mechanism worked when it could not work, then either the observation was wrong, or another factor existed among the known conditions.

Kael preferred the second possibility.

It had one pleasant property:

an additional factor could be found.

The first hour passed without incident.

Ollan examined what remained of the old connections and confirmed that there was no hidden power supply.

Ilsa checked the room for biological mana reactions.

Taven measured the building from inside and outside, comparing angles, height, and internal volume.

“Space is behaving itself,” he reported.

Mirael, standing by the entrance, nodded.

“That already makes it a good day.”

“I said behaving itself, not safe.”

“Taven.”

“Space is within tolerance.”

“Thank you.”

Meanwhile, Kael examined the residual signature.

Even a magical system that had been inactive for years left traces behind. Mana could linger in crystals, metal, or stone, especially if enormous flows had passed through the structure for years.

Here, however, the background was almost empty.

He ran a thin measuring needle along the damaged line.

The crystal in the handle barely changed colour.

“Residual mana is lower than expected even for a decommissioned station.”

Ollan, rummaging through an old cabinet, replied:

“Active corpse, though.”

“‘Dead circuit’ is acceptable as a colloquial designation.”

Ollan’s head emerged from behind the cabinet.

“Did I just receive official permission to shorten one of your terms?”

“You lost most of its precision.”

“But gained freedom.”

Ilsa called Noren over again.

She was reconstructing the sequence of events when, at one point, she asked a question no one had formulated before.

“Every time, did you notice the glow only after it had already started?”

The caretaker thought.

“The first time, yes. I heard the relay and went inside.”

“And the others?”

“The second time I was already in here. Checking the window. Then those two boys broke in, and it lit up almost immediately. Another time I came in after the rain that evening… Yes. Come to think of it, every time someone was here.”

Kael looked up.

“Before the activation?”

“Seems that way.”

He quickly opened his notebook and arranged the incidents in a table.

Five known activations.

In every case, someone had entered the room shortly before onset.

“You didn’t mention this before.”

Noren shrugged.

“No one asked where I was standing before the lamp came on.”

Kael wrote:

Possible activation dependence on presence of observer or biological source.

Ollan leaned over his shoulder.

“Please don’t call this a ‘response to the observer.’ I’d like to sleep tonight.”

“I wrote possible dependence.”

“Almost the same thing. Just looks scarier in a report.”

Mirael closed the folder.

“We test it under controlled conditions. One person at a time first, with no additional interference with the circuit. If anything changes outside the expected range, the experiment stops.”

Kael nodded.

“Reasonable.”

Ollan looked at him.

“You’re not going to argue?”

“With what?”

“I don’t know. I just wanted to record a rare natural phenomenon.”

By evening they had already performed several unsuccessful entries.

Mirael moved everyone outside, the room remained empty for about fifteen minutes, then Ollan and Ilsa entered one at a time.

Nothing happened.

Kael began to suspect that what mattered was not presence itself, but the duration of the empty interval, a particular individual, or some other parameter.

Then Noren offered to go in himself.

A few seconds later, something clicked inside the old wall.

Every conversation stopped.

The click came again, deeper this time, as if a relay had turned inside a cabinet that had been without power for years.

A thin blue-white light moved across the central plate.

It reached the first junction.

Then the second.

Then it continued onward.

Where the copper track no longer existed, the light did not stop.

At first Kael thought the line had simply jumped across the damaged section.

But it had not jumped anything.

The glow continued across bare stone, precisely following the former position of the dismantled copper.

Within seconds, the entire old circuit had appeared across the wall.

Complete.

Even the missing stabilising section manifested as a thin arc of light where only mounting holes remained.

“Measurements,” Mirael said sharply.

Ollan was already attaching a probe to one of the junctions.

“No incoming power.”

“Check the second range.”

“Checking.”

Ilsa raised a manometric detector.

“Background is almost unchanged. If the structure is receiving mana, the instruments aren’t detecting it.”

Taven stood by the wall with his spatial frame.

“Geometry stable.”

At that moment, the old inner door shuddered.

The mechanism, disconnected together with the rest of the station, drew its bolt back with a metallic scrape.

The door slammed shut.

Noren swore.

Kael did not take his eyes off the circuit.

The most disturbing thing was its lack of spectacle.

There was no flash, explosion, or visible deformation.

The circuit simply worked—calmly, routinely—as though twelve years of destruction and abandonment were someone’s clerical error.

Ollan changed instruments and again obtained a zero reading.

“Kael, it isn’t drawing energy.”

“The source may lie outside the range of mana detectors.”

“That’s three ranges already. And if this is direct conversion of archē, we should at least see a secondary trace.”

Kael took the instrument himself.

Zero.

And yet the mechanism was performing work.

The light remained active.

The relay had engaged.

The door had closed.

Every action required expenditure, yet none of the known expenditures were occurring.

Kael felt the familiar irritation that arose whenever a result existed without showing proper respect for the calculation.

“Self-sustaining functional reconstruction with an unidentified source of support under partial restoration of the original structural configuration…”

“You’re nervous,” Ilsa said.

Kael stopped.

“I’m refining the classification.”

“Your classifications get longer when you don’t understand what’s happening.”

“They get longer when the number of unknowns increases.”

Without looking away from his frame, Taven remarked:

“At the moment, those two statements are nearly equivalent.”

Kael was about to answer when the circuit went dark.

The light vanished from the entire wall at once.

The protective door released its bolt.

The room once again contained nothing but broken copper and dim evening light from the windows.

For several seconds, no one moved.

Noren was the first to break the silence.

“So what do you call it now?”

Ollan slowly put away his probe.

“I’m still voting for lamp.”

This time, Kael did not argue.

They managed to reproduce the activation twice more.

The group now knew that after a sufficiently long empty period, a person entering the room substantially increased the probability of activation.

But another detail proved more important.

An old plan of the station was found in the district archive, and Kael almost immediately noticed a discrepancy.

The luminous pattern did not reproduce the final operational configuration from before the station was shut down.

One side track sat lower than it did in the later documents.

Taven checked the dates.

“The plan is seventeen years old.”

“The circuit was modernised three years after this,” Kael said.

Ollan looked at the wall.

“So this thing chooses an older version?”

“Not necessarily chooses. We currently have no basis for attributing intention to the process.”

“Fine. This non-intentional thing, for some reason, prefers an older version.”

Kael ran a finger thoughtfully along the diagram.

If what they were seeing was an ordinary residual response, it would be more logical to expect the latest configuration, or at least the one that had been used most frequently.

Instead, the state manifesting here was one that had existed several years before the station’s final shutdown.

He stood.

“We need to determine what returns: only the visual structure, or the state of the component itself.”

Mirael immediately understood what he meant.

“Minimal damage. On an intact section. No interference with the central part.”

Ollan was already taking out a cutter.

“Sometimes you kill all the joy of science before it even begins.”

“Sometimes I send you home with the same number of limbs you arrived with.”

“That’s useful too.”

They selected a short section of copper line that was still in good condition.

Ollan carved a deep scratch into its surface.

Taven measured its position and length.

Ilsa wrote down the data as well.

Kael noticed.

“Medical component?”

“No. I want documentary evidence that you voluntarily damage state property.”

“The facility has been decommissioned.”

“Then you’re voluntarily damaging historical rubbish.”

“Controlled intervention in the system under investigation.”

Mirael smiled.

“Look, Ollan. I’m starting to understand their language.”

After another waiting period, Kael entered the room first.

At eight seconds, the relay clicked.

The circuit lit up.

Kael watched only the damaged section.

The scratch was gone.

For a moment, he did not fully comprehend what he was seeing.

The copper line shone smooth and unbroken, with no trace of the damage they had inflicted that day.

“Ollan.”

He came inside almost immediately.

“What?”

Kael pointed.

Ollan stopped.

“I made it right here.”

“I know.”

“Then where did it go?”

“That’s exactly what I’m trying to understand.”

Ollan instinctively reached toward the copper, but Mirael and Kael stopped him simultaneously.

“Don’t touch it.”

Everyone waited.

The circuit went dark.

The scratch returned.

It did not gradually appear.

It did not emerge from beneath the fading light.

In one moment, the surface was smooth.

In the next, the damage was back exactly where it had been.

Kael felt several convenient hypotheses collapse almost simultaneously.

It was not a simple light projection: the circuit performed work.

It was not repair: the damage returned when the effect ended.

It was not residual mana: there was practically none.

It was not an ordinary echo: an echo might repeat an old action, but it had no reason to negate the material’s new state.

“It isn’t repairing the circuit,” Kael said quietly.

Mirael stared at the returned scratch.

“Then what exactly is happening?”

Kael disliked that question when other people asked it.

He liked the answer he was now forced to give even less.

“I don’t know yet.”

Neither Ollan nor Ilsa made a joke.

By the end of the day, Mirael decided to suspend further experiments until they had obtained more archival data.

Noren locked the station.

Taven took the final spatial measurements.

The group was already preparing to leave when the cartographer called quietly:

“Kael.”

It was not the volume that made him turn.

It was the tone.

Over the course of their work together, Kael had learned to distinguish several versions of Taven saying his name.

One meant there was an error on the map.

Another meant someone was interfering with the measurements.

A third usually preceded Mirael saying something about immediate withdrawal.

This version, he had never heard before.

Taven stood outside at the edge of the old road, looking at the ground.

“What happened?”

He did not answer.

He merely pointed.

The evening sun was already descending behind the hills, stretching long shadows from the station, the bushes, and the old fence across the ground.

Among them lay another.

The tall, narrow shadow of a tower.

There was no tower beside it.

Kael slowly raised his eyes, as though the object might somehow be hiding above them.

Empty sky.

Field.

Road.

Back to the ground.

The shadow remained.

It had a straight shaft and a distinctive horizontal crossbar at the top.

Noren was the last to emerge from the building. When he saw it, he stopped on the threshold.

“No.”

Kael turned toward him.

“You recognise the shape?”

The caretaker was silent for a moment.

“There used to be a signal tower here. Before the line was rebuilt. They demolished it later.”

“When?”

“Along with the old station. About twelve years ago.”

Taven had already unfolded an archival site plan.

A tower of exactly that shape did indeed stand beside the building on the diagram.

Even a small projection on the upper crossbar matched the shadow.

Kael stepped closer.

The shadow began where the tower’s foundation had once stood and fell at the correct angle relative to the sun.

He suddenly realised that all their work that day had been organised around the wrong centre.

They had come because of the circuit.

But the circuit might merely have been the first thing to manifest strongly enough for someone to notice.

“A name?” Mirael asked.

Normally it took him a second.

Sometimes two.

Kael could already feel possibilities assembling in his mind:

residual optical fixation of a historical object;

archē-dependent optical reconstruction;

spatial preservation of previous geometry…

He rejected every one.

All of them described mechanisms he did not understand.

Kael looked at the shadow again.

“None yet.”

Ollan stood beside him and, for the first time all day, did not take the opportunity to make a joke.

That was more unsettling than the tower itself.

The group stayed overnight in Estel.

The local inn promised hot water, a hearty dinner, and quiet rooms.

Kael quickly discovered that the only true part of the description was the existence of rooms.

Late that evening, he sat at a small table beside the window, going through his notes.

Downstairs, the innkeeper was arguing with someone about the price of wine.

Through the wall came steady snoring.

Outside, the mana lamps went dark one by one.

Estel settled down to sleep with such tranquillity that one might have thought there was not a ruined station a few kilometres away that occasionally forgot its own condition.

Kael reread his first definition.

“Spontaneous reactivation of a physically disrupted mana circuit…”

Too narrow.

Nothing about the shadow.

He wrote another.

“Reconstruction of a previous functional state.”

Looked at it.

Crossed it out.

Reconstruction implied creation anew.

But nothing was being recreated.

The scratch did not heal.

The copper did not physically return.

The tower did not appear beside its shadow.

“Residual echo.”

Even worse.

An echo repeats what happened.

It gradually fades away.

It does not acquire the right to temporarily disregard damage inflicted today.

Kael put down the pencil and simply stared out the window for several minutes.

What they had observed did not resemble the return of the past.

The word return implied that the past had first genuinely left.

This felt different.

As though the previous state had ceased to be the current one, yet for some reason had not lost the right to present itself to the world again.

He picked up the pencil.

“Retention of a preceding state after its actual replacement by a subsequent one.”

Long.

And, more irritatingly, insufficiently precise.

Kael shortened it.

“Preceding-state retention effect.”

Then, in the margin, he wrote a single word:

Retention.

He looked at it for a while.

The term explained nothing, and that was exactly why it worked for now.

It did not describe a mechanism Kael did not know.

It did not attribute consciousness to the phenomenon.

It did not attempt to turn a hypothesis into an answer.

Simply:

retention.

Kael circled the word and turned the page.

At the beginning of his personal notebook was a list that Mirael had once called proof that excessive systematisation eventually became a distinct form of disease.

“Things It Is Still Too Early to Give a Proper Name.”

Number thirty-seven concerned the reason Ollan considered dried fish an acceptable breakfast.

Number thirty-eight concerned the ability of innkeepers to determine the temperature of water independently of the water’s actual physical state.

Kael added the next line.

“39. Retention anomaly of the Estel site.”

Below it, he added:

“Current designation describes the effect, not the mechanism. Do not classify as an ordinary residual phenomenon.”

He was about to close the notebook when he stopped.

Until that day, Kael had never considered it necessary to explicitly formulate the proposition that an event which had ended remained ended.

It had seemed as useless a sentence as:

A whole mug holds water better than a broken one.

Now there was a problem with it.

A trace could preserve what had happened.

A memory could describe what had happened.

Mana could carry the imprint of an action for a time.

But none of those things changed the fact that the present replaced the preceding state.

At Estel Station, something refused to accept that replacement as final.

Kael closed the notebook and sat in the darkness for a while longer.

Somewhere beyond the hills stood the old station.

On its wall was a broken circuit that sometimes returned to the shape it had possessed seventeen years earlier.

Beside it, no signal tower existed.

And yet the evening sun still found a shadow for one.

For the first time that day, Kael came up with a formulation he had no intention of putting into any official document.

For some reason, what had already ended had not yet gone away.
